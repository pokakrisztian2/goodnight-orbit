> Corrections to the brief:
> - **The ultraviolet catastrophe did not drive Planck.** He was fitting new infrared measurements by Rubens and Kurlbaum (Rubens brought them to tea at the Plancks' on Sunday 7 October 1900). Rayleigh's classical result "played no part at all in the earliest phase," the full Rayleigh–Jeans formula is from 1905, and Ehrenfest coined "ultraviolet catastrophe" in 1911. Kragh calls the textbook story "closer to a fairytale than to historical truth" (Kragh PW). Narrate the catastrophe as the puzzle classical physics *would* have faced, not as Planck's motive.
> - **Late Nobels:** Planck's 1918 prize was handed over in 1919, Einstein's 1921 prize in 1922, and Heisenberg's 1932 prize on 11 December 1933. Say "the 1918 prize," etc.
> - **"Photon" is not Einstein's word.** He said "light quantum." Gilbert N. Lewis coined "photon" in December 1926, for a slightly different idea.
> - **Davisson and Germer were not testing de Broglie.** An equipment accident in 1925 recrystallised their nickel, and Davisson only heard of de Broglie's theory in summer 1926.
> - **Double-slit firsts:** the first electron double slit was Jönsson (Tübingen, 1961). The first dot-by-dot single-electron build-up was Merli, Missiroli and Pozzi (Bologna, 1974/1976). Tonomura's Hitachi experiment (1989) is the famous, cleanest version. When Feynman lectured (1963) it was still a thought experiment, and he said so.
> - **Heisenberg's paper was received on 29 July 1925,** not 29 June (a slip on AIP's older chronology page). AIP (2025) treats the Helgoland story as partly myth: he was there about 10 days, and much of the work came before and after.
> - **Uncertainty:** Heisenberg's own 1927 paper *did* use the "measuring disturbs it" picture (the gamma-ray microscope), and Bohr corrected him. The modern form (Kennard 1927) is about the spread built into the state. Say "the principle isn't really about clumsy measurement," not "Heisenberg said it wasn't."
> - **Solvay 1927:** the Bohr–Einstein duel happened mostly over breakfast and dinner, not in the formal sessions (BV). The famous photon-box argument was at Solvay 1930.
> - **"God does not play dice"** paraphrases Einstein's letter to Born of 4 December 1926 ("I am convinced that He does not throw dice"). **"Einstein, stop telling God what to do"** has no primary source; use Bohr's own 1949 account instead.
> - **Feynman's "nobody understands quantum mechanics"** is from his Messenger Lectures at **Cornell**, 9–19 November 1964 (book: *The Character of Physical Law*, 1965). Some web copies wrongly say MIT.
> - **Bell's 1964 paper** came out of a year's leave from CERN in the US (Stanford, Wisconsin, Brandeis), per the CERN archive. It appeared on 4 November 1964 in a journal that lasted only 1964–68.
> - **"Aspect 1982" is three papers** (1981, mid-1982, December 1982). Only the last switched settings while the photons were in flight, and even that was not truly random. The first real Bell test was Clauser–Freedman (1972); fully loophole-free tests came in 2015–17.
> - **Entanglement cannot send messages faster than light** (Aspect, in his Nobel interview).
> - **Stern–Gerlach (1922) was not recognised as spin at the time.** Spin was proposed in October 1925 and linked to the experiment only in 1927.
> - **"Why you don't fall through the floor":** word it as "without the exclusion principle, matter would collapse" (Dyson & Lenard 1967). How much of the floor's push is electric repulsion and how much is Pauli is genuinely subtle.
> - **Google's claims come in pairs:** 2019 was 200 seconds vs 10,000 years (IBM answered: 2.5 days). Willow (Dec 2024) gave (a) error correction "below threshold," peer-reviewed in *Nature*, and (b) a separate benchmark, under 5 minutes vs 10²⁵ years, which Google says "has no known real-world applications." Don't merge them.
> - **The laser Nobel (1964) went to Townes, Basov and Prokhorov,** not to Maiman, who built the first laser. **Rutherford's Nobel (1908) was in chemistry,** before the nucleus.
> - **Chapter split:** the old chapter 11 was overloaded, so it is now 11 (spin, Pauli, tunnelling), 12 (everyday technology) and 13 (quantum computers + closing).

## 0. Intro — the words we will use (plain-English toolkit)

- **Quantum physics** "is the study of matter and energy at the most fundamental level." — Caltech QP
- The word **quantum** "has Latin roots and means 'how much.'" — Caltech QP
- **Quanta:** "matter and energy can be thought of as discrete packets, or quanta, that have a minimum value associated with them." — Caltech QP
- **Photon:** "light of a fixed frequency will deliver energy in quanta called 'photons.' Each photon at this frequency will have the same amount of energy, and this energy can't be broken down into smaller units." — Caltech QP
- **Planck's constant (h)** is the size of the energy step: a resonator of frequency ν can only have energy in lumps of hν. — Nobel biography of Planck: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1918/planck/biographical/
- Since 2019 the value of h is fixed exactly: 6.626 070 15 × 10⁻³⁴ joule-seconds, with zero uncertainty. — NIST CODATA: https://physics.nist.gov/cgi-bin/cuu/Value?h
- In November 2018, "a group of 60 nations voted to redefine the kilogram in terms of an invariant of nature: the Planck constant." (The new definition took effect on 20 May 2019.) So the kilogram in your kitchen scales now rests on quantum physics. — NIST: https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/kilogram **UNCERTAIN (the 20 May 2019 start date is standard but was not on the page we read)**
- **Energy levels / orbitals:** electrons in atoms sit in orbitals, "mathematical descriptions that represent the probability of the electrons' existence." "Electrons can jump from one orbital to another as they gain or lose energy, but they cannot be found between orbitals." — Caltech QP
- **Wave–particle duality:** light and matter showed "the properties of particles or waves, depending on how they were measured… they are actually neither particle nor wave. They are distinct quantum objects that we cannot easily conceptualize." — Caltech QP
- **Superposition:** "a combination of multiple possible states at the same time… analogous to a ripple on the surface of a pond that is a combination of two waves overlapping." — Caltech QP
- **Wavefunction and probability (the Born rule, in plain words):** quantum objects are described by "wave functions," solutions of the Schrödinger equation, which "provide the likelihoods of where an observer might find a particular object." — Caltech QP
- **Measurement:** "the term 'observation' is misleading… suggesting that consciousness is involved. Instead, 'measurement' better describes the effect," an interaction with the environment, including the measuring device. "A full understanding of the relationship between measurement and outcome is still needed." — Caltech QP
- **Uncertainty principle:** "a trade-off between complementary points of view… two properties of an object, such as its position and velocity, cannot both be precisely known at the same time." — Caltech QP
- **Entanglement:** two or more objects "connected in such a way that they can be thought of as a single system, even if they are very far apart." — Caltech QP
- **Spin** is the electron's built-in ("internal") angular momentum, discovered a few years after the 1922 Stern–Gerlach experiment that first split a beam of silver atoms in two. — Quanta Magazine (2023): https://www.quantamagazine.org/the-often-overlooked-experiment-that-revealed-the-quantum-world-20231205/
- **Tunnelling:** "a phenomenon in which particles penetrate a potential energy barrier with a height greater than the total energy of the particles." It "is important in models of the Sun." — OpenStax *University Physics Vol. 3*, §7.6: https://openstax.org/books/university-physics-volume-3/pages/7-6-the-quantum-tunneling-of-particles-through-potential-barriers
- **Qubit:** "can act like a bit, storing either a zero or a one, but it can also exist as a weighted combination of both simultaneously." Two qubits can be in a superposition of all four 2-bit strings. — IBM QC
- **Decoherence** (IBM's working definition): "the process in which a system in a quantum state collapses into a nonquantum state," triggered by measurement or by the environment. — IBM QC. (Philosophers are more careful: see SEP note in chapter 9.)

## 1. A Glowing Problem (black-body radiation, Planck 1900)

- A **black body** is an object that absorbs all light falling on it. Heat one and it glows with a spectrum that depends only on its temperature, not on what it is made of. — Nobel facts, Planck: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1918/planck/facts/
- Using the physics of the day, "the radiation in the high-frequency area of the spectrum became infinite." — Nobel facts, Planck
- The study began in 1859 with Gustav Kirchhoff, who was Planck's predecessor as professor of physics in Berlin. — Kragh PW
- Planck was born in Kiel on 23 April 1858 and succeeded Kirchhoff at Berlin University in 1889. — Nobel biography, Planck
- Planck was "deeply interested in – even obsessed with – the second law of thermodynamics" (the law that entropy, disorder, never decreases). — Kragh PW
- In 1896 Wilhelm Wien found a radiation law that fitted the precise measurements at the Physikalisch-Technische Reichsanstalt in Berlin. — Kragh PW
- In 1899 Planck found a derivation of Wien's law and "was satisfied." Then "experiments performed in Berlin showed that the Wien-Planck law did not correctly describe the spectrum at very low frequencies." — Kragh PW
- **The tea visit:** on Sunday 7 October 1900 the experimenter Heinrich Rubens and his wife visited the Plancks. Rubens and Ferdinand Kurlbaum had measured black-body radiation at the long infrared wavelength of 51 microns, and it did not fit Wien's law. — JQI (Univ. of Maryland), "Planck and the Birth of Quantum Mechanics": https://jqi.umd.edu/news/planck-and-birth-quantum-mechanics
- At low frequencies, Rubens told him, the measured energy rose in a straight line with temperature. "That evening he figured out the shape of the curve." — JQI (above)
- The story that Planck mailed the formula to Rubens on a postcard that same evening comes from Abraham Pais's book *Subtle is the Lord*. — JQI (above) **UNCERTAIN (anecdote via Pais)**
- Planck presented the new radiation law to the German Physical Society on 19 October 1900. — Kragh PW; Max Planck Society: https://www.mpg.de/19252712/max-plancks-private-life ; Mukunda. (JQI gives 17 October; 19 October is the date in the other sources.)
- That first version "agreed perfectly with the experimental spectrum in the lower-frequency infrared region" but had no energy steps in it yet. — Kragh PW
- To derive it properly, Planck turned to Boltzmann's statistical idea of entropy, which he had resisted for years, and split the energy into "energy elements." — Kragh PW
- **14 December 1900:** at the German Physical Society he treated energy "as made up of a completely determinate number of finite equal parts, and for this purpose I use the constant of nature h = 6.55 × 10⁻²⁷ (erg sec)." — Kragh PW (quoting Planck)
- **The staircase:** a resonator of frequency ν can only take energy in steps of hν, the rule E = hν. Before, energy was thought to flow like a ramp; now it came in stair steps. — Nobel biography, Planck; Mukunda
- The steps are tiny. With h ≈ 6.6 × 10⁻³⁴ J·s, one step of green light (about 5.5 × 10¹⁴ vibrations per second) is about 3.6 × 10⁻¹⁹ joules. That is why everyday energy looks smooth. — computed from NIST's value of h
- Planck was 42 years old in December 1900 (born April 1858). — Nobel biography, Planck
- **Reluctance:** Planck later called the quanta "a purely formal assumption and I really did not give it much thought except that no matter what the cost, I must bring about a positive result." — Kragh PW (quoting Planck's 1931 letter)
- "If a revolution occurred in physics in December 1900, nobody seemed to notice it. Planck was no exception." — Kragh PW
- Between 1901 and 1906 Planck published nothing on black-body radiation or quantum theory. Only around 1908 did he accept that the quantum was something classical physics could not explain. — Kragh PW
- "Like Copernicus, Planck became a revolutionary against his will." — Kragh PW
- In 1911 Planck said: "The hypothesis of quanta will never vanish from the world." — Kragh PW
- The Max Planck Society's own headline for 19 October 1900: "a revolution in physics begins unnoticed." — MPG: https://www.mpg.de/19252712/max-plancks-private-life
- **The ultraviolet catastrophe:** in June 1900 Lord Rayleigh showed that classical physics gave an energy that grows with the square of the frequency, "utterly in conflict with the data." Rayleigh and Jeans gave the full formula in 1905; Jeans fixed a missing factor of 8 in June 1905. It predicts endless energy at high frequencies, "catastrophic" in the ultraviolet. — Kragh PW; Mukunda
- The name "ultraviolet catastrophe" was coined by Paul Ehrenfest in 1911. — Kragh PW
- **Correction to the popular story:** the ultraviolet catastrophe "played no part at all in the earliest phase of quantum theory." Planck was answering new infrared measurements, not the catastrophe. Kragh calls the textbook version "closer to a fairytale than to historical truth." — Kragh PW
- Einstein, in his 1905 light-quantum paper, was the first to show that the Rayleigh–Jeans law is what classical physics truly predicts. "Amusingly he did it before Jeans." — Mukunda
- Some historians (Thomas Kuhn, Olivier Darrigol) argue that Planck did not really mean energy was chopped into steps in 1900, and that Einstein saw it first. Kragh calls this "controversial" and much debated. — Kragh PW
- 1918 Nobel Prize in Physics to Max Planck, "in recognition of the services he rendered to the advancement of Physics by his discovery of energy quanta." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1918/summary/
- The 1918 prize was reserved (no nomination met the criteria in 1918) and handed to Planck one year later, in 1919. — Nobel 1918 summary (above)
- The first Solvay conference (Brussels, 1911) was on "radiation theory and the quanta." It marked the take-off of quantum theory. Planck and Einstein both attended. — Kragh PW

## 2. Light Comes in Packets (Einstein 1905, photoelectric effect)

- **Callback to video 1:** Einstein was a patent clerk in Bern. He was appointed Technical Expert Class III (on trial) at 3,500 francs a year and started in June 1902 (IGE says July). — Swiss Federal Institute of Intellectual Property: https://www.ige.ch/en/about-us/the-history-of-the-ipi/einstein/einstein-at-the-patent-office
- He split his days into eight hours of patent work, eight hours of "allotria" (his own physics) and eight hours of sleep. — IGE (above)
- He called the office "that worldly cloister where I hatched my most beautiful ideas." — IGE (above)
- The paper "On a heuristic point of view concerning the production and transformation of light" was received by *Annalen der Physik* on 18 March 1905 and published in vol. 17, pp. 132–148. It was the first of his 1905 papers. — Mukunda
- "Heuristic" means a helpful way of looking at things, a working guess, not a finished theory.
- The key sentence: "the energy of a light ray spreading out from a point source is not continuously distributed over an increasing space but consists of a finite number of energy quanta which are localized at points in space, which move without dividing, and which can only be produced and absorbed as complete units." — Einstein 1905, p. 133, English translation quoted in arXiv:1811.04509: https://arxiv.org/pdf/1811.04509
- Einstein to Conrad Habicht, May 1905: the paper "deals with radiation and the energy properties of light and is very revolutionary." — Mukunda
- Of all his 1905 papers, this is the one Einstein himself called revolutionary. — Mukunda
- In 1887 Heinrich Hertz saw that ultraviolet light shining on metal makes sparks jump more easily. J. J. Thomson later showed that the light knocks out electrons. — Mukunda
- **Lenard's puzzle:** in 1902 Philipp Lenard found that the energy of the knocked-out electrons showed "not the slightest dependence on the light intensity," even when the light was made a thousand times brighter. Their energy rose with the light's frequency (its colour) instead. Waves could not explain this. — Mukunda
- **The packet picture:** a light packet of energy hν hits one electron and gives it all its energy. The electron pays a fixed "exit fee" W to leave the metal: E = hν − W. Brighter light means more packets and more electrons, not faster ones. — Mukunda
- Below a certain frequency no electrons come out at all, however bright the light. "One such light quantum, a photon, must have a certain minimum frequency before it can liberate an electron." — Nobel facts, Einstein: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/einstein/facts/
- Red light, however bright, cannot free electrons from many metals. Faint ultraviolet can. (Plain-English example of the rule above.) — Nobel facts, Einstein
- **Millikan's test:** Robert Millikan spent about ten years testing Einstein's equation. His 1916 *Physical Review* paper confirmed it and measured h as 6.57 × 10⁻²⁷ erg-seconds to "a precision of about 0.5 per cent." — APS Physics, "Quantum Milestones, 1916": https://physics.aps.org/articles/v18/12
- He used "a machine shop in vacuo," a rotating knife worked by magnets from outside the vacuum, to shave the metal clean before each shot of light. — APS Physics (above)
- Millikan still did not believe in light particles. In 1912 he called a particle theory of light "quite unthinkable." — APS Physics (above)
- In 1916 he called the photon "the bold, not to say the reckless, hypothesis." — APS Physics (above); Mukunda
- In his Nobel lecture he said the work had "resulted, contrary to my own expectation, in the first direct experimental proof… of the Einstein equation." — APS Physics (above)
- Millikan won the 1923 Nobel "for his work on the elementary charge of electricity and on the photoelectric effect." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1923/millikan/facts/
- Einstein's Nobel Prize is the 1921 prize, "for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect." It was not for relativity. — Nobel facts, Einstein
- Like Planck's, it was reserved and given one year late, in 1922. — Nobel facts, Einstein
- **The word "photon"** was coined by the American chemist Gilbert N. Lewis in a letter to *Nature* dated 18 December 1926, "The conservation of photons." Einstein had said "light quantum" (*Lichtquant*). — APS News, This Month in Physics History: https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2012/12/gilbert-lewis-coins-photon-letter
- Lewis meant something a little different from Einstein's idea: "I therefore take the liberty of proposing for this hypothetical new atom, which is not light but plays an essential part in every process of radiation, the name photon." His theory faded; his word stayed. — APS News (above)

## 3. The Atom's Staircase (Rutherford 1911, Bohr 1913, spectral lines)

- In 1897 J. J. Thomson showed that cathode rays are made of particles, electrons, and concluded that electrons are part of atoms. — Nobel facts, J. J. Thomson: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1906/thomson/facts/
- In 1909, at Manchester, Hans Geiger was training a young undergraduate, Ernest Marsden. Rutherford suggested Marsden check whether any alpha particles bounce back from thin metal foil. — Univ. of Virginia physics notes (quoting Rutherford's later lecture): https://galileo.phys.virginia.edu/classes/252/Rutherford_Scattering/Rutherford_Scattering.html
- The counting was done by eye: tiny flashes on a zinc-sulphide screen in complete darkness. One person could only count accurately for about a minute at a time. — UVA (above)
- Rutherford: "It was quite the most incredible event that ever happened to me in my life. It was almost as incredible as if you fired a 15-inch shell at a piece of tissue paper and it came back and hit you." — UVA (above). Usually traced to a 1936 Cambridge lecture, printed in *Background to Modern Science* (1938), or to A. S. Eve's 1939 biography. **UNCERTAIN (the lecture's date and place vary between sources; the wording is stable)**
- The gold foil was "beaten into leaf about 400 atoms thick." — UVA (above)
- From this Rutherford proposed the nucleus: "practically the whole mass of the atom and at the same time all positive charge of the atom is concentrated in a minute space at the centre." — Nobel biography, Rutherford: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1908/rutherford/biographical/
- Rutherford's own Nobel (1908) was in **chemistry**, "for his investigations into the disintegration of the elements, and the chemistry of radioactive substances." That was before the nucleus. — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/chemistry/1908/rutherford/facts/
- **The problem:** in ordinary physics an electron circling a nucleus should give off light all the time, lose energy and spiral inward. Atoms should not be stable. Bohr's paper says his second assumption is "in obvious contrast to the ordinary ideas of electrodynamics, but appears to be necessary in order to account for experimental facts." — Bohr 1913, p. 7
- Niels Bohr (born Copenhagen, 7 October 1885) worked in Rutherford's Manchester laboratory in spring 1912. — Nobel biography, Bohr: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1922/bohr/biographical/
- Bohr's paper "On the Constitution of Atoms and Molecules" came in three parts in the *Philosophical Magazine*, 1913 (vol. 26: pp. 1–25 in July, 476–502 and 857–875 later that year), often called the "trilogy." — Bohr 1913; Taylor & Francis: https://www.tandfonline.com/doi/abs/10.1080/14786441308634955
- Its first words: "In order to explain the results of experiments on scattering of α rays by matter Prof. Rutherford has given a theory of the structure of atoms." — Bohr 1913
- **Bohr's two main assumptions:** (1) electrons in "stationary states" follow ordinary mechanics, but the jump between states does not; (2) a jump sends out light of one frequency, with energy and frequency linked by "Planck's theory." — Bohr 1913, p. 7
- In plain words: the electron lives only on certain steps of a staircase. When it drops from a higher step to a lower one, it sends out a photon whose energy is exactly the height of the drop. — Nobel facts, Bohr: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1922/bohr/facts/
- "Bohr's model explained why atoms only emit light of fixed wavelengths." — Nobel facts, Bohr
- **Balmer:** in 1885 Johann Jakob Balmer, a mathematics teacher at a girls' secondary school in Basel, found a simple formula that fit the wavelengths of the visible hydrogen lines. — MacTutor, St Andrews: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Balmer/
- Nobody knew why Balmer's formula worked until Bohr's 1913 theory. Bohr: letting the lower step be fixed and the upper one vary, "we get the ordinary Balmer series." — Bohr 1913; MacTutor (above)
- A lovely detail from the 1913 paper: in laboratory tubes, no more than 12 Balmer lines had been seen, "while 33 lines are observed in the spectra of some celestial bodies." Bohr explained why: the high orbits are huge and need very thin gas, like the gas of stars. — Bohr 1913, p. 9
- **Neon signs:** in a gas discharge tube, electrons in the gas atoms "first become excited, and then fall back to lower energy levels, emitting light of a distinctive color." — Chemistry LibreTexts (CK-12), "Atomic Emission Spectra": https://chem.libretexts.org/Bookshelves/Introductory_Chemistry/Introductory_Chemistry_(CK-12)/05:_Electrons_in_Atoms/5.05:_Atomic_Emission_Spectra
- "Only signs that glow with the red-orange color… are actually filled with neon. Signs of other colors contain different gases or mixtures of gases." — LibreTexts (above)
- Hydrogen's glow, passed through a prism, splits into four visible lines of very specific wavelengths. "Every element has a unique atomic emission spectrum," like a fingerprint. — LibreTexts (above)
- 1922 Nobel Prize in Physics to Niels Bohr "for his services in the investigation of the structure of atoms and of the radiation emanating from them." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1922/summary/
- Bohr's 1922 prize and Einstein's reserved 1921 prize were announced together in November 1922. — Nobel facts, Einstein; Nobel 1922 summary **UNCERTAIN (same-day announcement is well known but not stated on these two pages)**
- Bohr's orbits were later replaced. Today electrons are pictured in orbitals, clouds of probability, not little planets. — Caltech QP

## 4. Waves That Are Particles (de Broglie 1924, Davisson–Germer 1927, G. P. Thomson)

- Louis de Broglie was born in Dieppe on 15 August 1892, "the son of Victor, Duc de Broglie." The Nobel committee's own citation names him "Prince Louis-Victor Pierre Raymond de Broglie." — Nobel biography: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1929/broglie/biographical/ ; Nobel 1929 summary: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1929/summary/
- He "applied himself first to literary studies and took his degree in history in 1910." Then, "as his liking for science prevailed," he took a science degree in 1913. — Nobel biography, de Broglie
- In World War I he served in the army's wireless section, "stationed at the Eiffel Tower." — Nobel biography, de Broglie
- His elder brother Maurice was an experimental physicist (X-rays), and Louis took an interest in his work. — Nobel biography, de Broglie
- At 19 Louis went with Maurice to the first Solvay conference (1911) as his secretary. Reading the minutes, he wrote later: "I had promised myself to devote all my efforts to understanding the true nature of the mysterious quanta." — Rocci & Lambert, "The Solvay Councils, de Broglie's brothers…," arXiv:2411.18524: https://arxiv.org/abs/2411.18524
- In 1924 he defended his doctoral thesis, *Recherches sur la Théorie des Quanta* (Researches on the quantum theory), at the University of Paris. — Nobel biography, de Broglie
- **The big idea:** "In 1924 Louis de Broglie introduced the idea that particles, such as electrons, could be described not only as particles but also as waves." — Nobel facts: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1929/broglie/facts/
- The rule is λ = h/p: wavelength equals Planck's constant divided by momentum. Heavier or faster things have shorter waves. — Davisson, Nobel lecture (1937): https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/davisson-lecture.pdf
- The Nobel presenter in 1929: "At a time when no single known fact supported this theory, Louis de Broglie asserted that a stream of electrons which passed through a very small hole in an opaque screen must exhibit the same phenomena as a light ray." — Presentation speech, C. - They began searching in autumn 1926. Early in 1927 they found diffraction beams "first one and then twenty others in rapid succession." In every usable case "the correctness of the de Broglie formula, λ = h/p was verified." — Davisson, Nobel lecture

- **G. P. Thomson** at Aberdeen sent electrons through very thin metal films, "which showed that electrons behave as waves in spite of being particles." — Nobel biography, G. P. Thomson: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1937/thomson/biographical/
- 1937 Nobel Prize in Physics jointly to Davisson and G. P. Thomson "for their experimental discovery of the diffraction of electrons by crystals." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1937/summary/
- **Father and son:** G. P. Thomson was the son of J. J. Thomson, the Nobel winner "more than anyone else… responsible for the discovery of the electron." ## 5. The One Experiment (double slit; Feynman; Jönsson 1961, Merli–Missiroli–Pozzi 1976, Tonomura 1989)

- Thomas Young gave the Royal Society's Bakerian Lecture, "Experiments and Calculations Relative to Physical Optics," on 24 November 1803 (printed in *Phil. Trans.* 94, 1804). It was his strongest case for light as a wave. — Royal Society: https://royalsocietypublishing.org/doi/10.1098/rstl.1804.0001 (page blocks curl; details via search results); Fortnightly Review: https://fortnightlyreview.co.uk/2014/09/thomas-young/

- Feynman chose this experiment to open his quantum volume: "We choose to examine a phenomenon which is impossible, absolutely impossible, to explain in any classical way, and which has in it the heart of quantum mechanics. In reality, it contains the only mystery. We cannot make the mystery go away by 'explaining' how it works. We will just tell you how it works." — Feynman III-1, §1-1 (grepped from PDF); the same quote is reproduced by Physics World (2002): https://physicsworld.com/a/the-double-slit-experiment/
- A year later, in his Messenger Lectures at Cornell (9–19 November 1964, filmed by the BBC), Feynman told a general audience: "…a lot of people understood the theory of relativity in some way or other, certainly more than twelve. On the other hand, I think I can safely say that nobody understands quantum mechanics." — transcript of lecture 6, "Probability and Uncertainty," Georgetown Univ. copy: https://bouman.chem.georgetown.edu/general/feynman.html (the page labels it "MIT", which is wrong); Cornell dates: Open Culture: https://www.openculture.com/2021/12/the-character-of-physical-law-richard-feynmans-legendary-course-presented-at-cornell-1964.html
- The line is a callback to relativity (video 1): he had just joked about the old newspaper claim that only twelve people understood Einstein.
- His advice to the audience fits a sleep video perfectly: "So do not take the lecture too seriously, feeling that you really have to understand in terms of some model what I am going to describe, but just relax and enjoy it. I am going to tell you what nature behaves like. If you will simply admit that maybe she does behave like this, you will find her a delightful, entrancing thing." — same transcript
- And: "Do not keep saying to yourself, if you can possibly avoid it, 'But how can it be like that?' because you will get 'down the drain', into a blind alley from which nobody has escaped. Nobody knows how it can be like that." — same transcript (the web copy has the typo "possible")
- Feynman's bullets: "bullets always arrive in lumps, and when we find something in the detector, it is always one whole bullet." — Feynman III-1, §1-2
- Feynman's electrons: "We shall say: 'Electrons always arrive in identical lumps.'" — Feynman III-1, §1-4
- Feynman was frank that, in 1963, this was a thought experiment: "This experiment has never been done in just this way. The trouble is that the apparatus would have to be made on an impossibly small scale." — Feynman III-1, §1-4
- Watching changes the result: "When we watch them, the electrons come through just as we would expect them to come through" — like bullets, with no interference. — Feynman III-1, §1-6
- Feynman's summary rule: if an experiment can tell which way was taken, "the probability of the event is the sum of the probabilities for each alternative. The interference is lost." — Feynman III-1, §1-7
- Feynman ties this to Heisenberg: "It is impossible to design an apparatus to determine which hole the electron passes through, that will not at the same time disturb the electrons enough to destroy the interference pattern." And: "No one has ever found (or even thought of) a way around the uncertainty principle." — Feynman III-1, §1-8
- The first real double-slit experiment with electrons was done in 1961 by Claus Jönsson of Tübingen (*Zeitschrift für Physik* 161, 454). He showed interference with up to five slits. — Physics World (2002): https://physicsworld.com/a/the-double-slit-experiment/
- Jönsson's slits were about 3 × 10⁻⁷ m wide (0.3 micrometres). — Rosa, "The Merli–Missiroli–Pozzi Two-Slit Electron-Interference Experiment," *Physics in Perspective* (2012): https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC4617474/

- Their 16 mm film *Interferenza di elettroni* won first prize at the VII Scientific and Technical Cinema Festival in Brussels in 1976. — Rosa (PMC, above)
- In 1989 Akira Tonomura's team at Hitachi published "Demonstration of single-electron buildup of an interference pattern," using an electron microscope with an "electron biprism" instead of two slits. — Tonomura 1989
- The biprism is two parallel plates with a very fine filament in the middle, "thinner than 1 micron (1/1000 mm)." Electrons pass on both sides of the filament. — Hitachi: https://www.hitachi.com/rd/research/materials/quantum/doubleslit/index.html
- The pictures in the 1989 paper show the screen after 10, 100, 3,000, 20,000 and 70,000 electrons. At first the dots look random. The fringes appear only as the dots pile up. — Tonomura 1989, Fig. 5 caption
- In the final picture, about 70,000 electrons had arrived, "i.e., 14 000 electrons per fringe." — Tonomura 1989, p. 119
- The paper gives a rate of about 1,000 electrons per second over the whole field of view, and says the fringes form "in a reasonable time, say, 20 min." — Tonomura 1989, p. 119. (Hitachi's web page instead says "only 10 electrons are emitted per second." Use "a trickle of electrons" or "a few at a time" to be safe.)
- On average, one electron was about 150 km behind the one before it, while the microscope was only 1.5 m long. So there was almost never more than one electron in the machine at once. — Tonomura 1989, p. 119
- Hitachi: the electrons were accelerated with 50,000 volts and moved at about 40% of the speed of light, "120,000 km/second… These electrons can go around the earth three times in a second." — Hitachi page (above)
- Hitachi's own summary: "Although electrons were sent one by one, interference fringes could be observed… Whenever electrons are observed, they are always detected as individual particles. When accumulated, however, interference fringes are formed." — Hitachi page (above)
- Tonomura's conclusion: they "realized a two-slit interference experiment, once regarded as a pure thought experiment." Their first footnote cites Feynman's Lectures. — Tonomura 1989, p. 120
- In 2002, Robert Crease asked *Physics World* readers to name the most beautiful experiment in physics. More than 200 replied. The winner was "Young's double-slit experiment applied to the interference of single electrons." — Physics World (2002): https://physicsworld.com/a/the-double-slit-experiment/ ; Rosa (PMC, above)
- Tonomura's 1989 paper did not cite the Bologna group's 1976 paper (its footnote 4 mentions only their movie: "The movie was produced by G. Pozzi and G. F. Missiroli"). This led to letters and corrections in *Physics World* in 2003. — Rosa (PMC, above); Tonomura 1989 footnotes
- Light does it too, one photon at a time. In 1986 Philippe Grangier, Gérard Roger and Alain Aspect showed that a true single photon goes only one way at a beam splitter, yet single photons still build up interference with visibility above 98%. — *Europhysics Letters* 1, 173 (1986): https://iopscience.iop.org/article/10.1209/0295-5075/1/4/004 (details via search summary) **UNCERTAIN (paper not read directly; details via search summary)**
- The "one at a time" idea is older: G. I. Taylor's 1909 paper "Interference fringes with feeble light" used very dim light. — same search source **UNCERTAIN (Taylor's light was dim, but not reliably one photon at a time; say "very faint light")**
- Big molecules do it too. ## 6. A Summer on an Island (Heisenberg 1925, matrix mechanics, uncertainty 1927)

- Werner Heisenberg was born on 5 December 1901 in Würzburg. — AIP Heisenberg, brief chronology
- On 6 June 1925, a "swollen-faced, stuffy-nosed" Heisenberg, aged 23 and suffering from hay fever, left for the North Sea island of Helgoland. — APS News (July 2025): https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2025/07/werner-heisenberg-pioneers-quantum-mechanics
- He asked Max Born for two weeks' leave from Göttingen. Helgoland lies about 50 km off the German coast and is barely 1 km² in size. — Physics World (2025): https://physicsworld.com/a/return-to-helgoland-celebrating-100-years-of-quantum-mechanics/
- Heisenberg's own later account: "it was almost three o'clock in the morning before the final result of my computations lay before me… I was far too excited to sleep and so, as a new day dawned, I made for the southern tip of the island, where I had been longing to climb a rock jutting out into the sea. I now did so without too much trouble, and waited for the sun to rise." — Heisenberg, *Physics and Beyond* (1971; German *Der Teil und das Ganze*, 1969), quoted in Physics World (2025, above)
- Historians are careful here. AIP: "Contemporary evidence confirms that Heisenberg spent about 10 days on Helgoland in June 1925. It's not clear how much he accomplished there." He "probably did much of the work… before and after his brief visit." — AIP (June 2025): https://www.aip.org/history/what-happened-on-helgoland
- AIP says the popular Helgoland tale "seems to stem from Heisenberg himself," above all his 1969 memoirs. — AIP (above)
- His idea: stop trying to picture electron orbits that no one can see. He wrote to Pauli in 1925: "All of my meagre efforts go toward killing off and suitably replacing the concept of the orbital path which one cannot observe." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Quantum Mechanic"
- The paper's own aim: "to establish a basis for theoretical quantum mechanics founded exclusively upon relationships between quantities which in principle are observable." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Quantum Mechanic"
- The paper, "Über quantentheoretische Umdeutung kinematischer und mechanischer Beziehungen" (*Z. Phys.* 33, 879–893), was received on 29 July 1925. — AIP (Helgoland article, above); Aitchison, MacManus & Snyder, arXiv:quant-ph/0404009: https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0404009 (AIP's older chronology page says "29 June"; 29 July is correct)
- Heisenberg gave the paper to Born and left on a month-long trip to Holland and England and a camping trip. Born puzzled over the strange maths and realised the tables of numbers were "matrices." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Quantum Mechanic"
- Born, in his Nobel lecture: "after a week of intensive thought and trial I suddenly remembered an algebraic theory which I had learned from my teacher, Professor Rosanes, in Breslau. Such square arrays are well known to mathematicians… and are called matrices." — Born, Nobel Lecture (1954): https://www.nobelprize.org/uploads/2018/06/born-lecture.pdf
- Born and Pascual Jordan then wrote up matrix mechanics, and Born, Heisenberg and Jordan sent in the "three-man paper" on 16 November 1925. — AIP (Helgoland article); AIP Heisenberg
- Steven Weinberg admitted: "I have tried several times to read the paper that Heisenberg wrote on returning from Heligoland, and, although I think I understand quantum mechanics, I have never understood Heisenberg's motivations for the mathematical steps in his paper… Heisenberg's 1925 paper was pure magic." — quoted in Aitchison et al., arXiv:quant-ph/0404009
- The uncertainty paper, "Über den anschaulichen Inhalt der quantentheoretischen Kinematik und Mechanik" (*Z. Phys.* 43, 172–198), was received on 23 March 1927. — AIP Heisenberg, brief chronology; SEP Uncertainty
- Heisenberg worked it out in February 1927 as a lecturer at Bohr's institute in Copenhagen, while Bohr was away skiing. He first set it out in a 14-page letter to Pauli. — AIP Heisenberg, "Triumph" and "Uncertainty Principle" pages
- Heisenberg's own example was a "gamma-ray microscope": to see an electron sharply you need very short-wave light, but that light kicks the electron and changes its momentum. — SEP Uncertainty
- Bohr objected that the microscope argument was flawed, though the result was right. Heisenberg added an "Addition in proof" to the paper admitting the error. — AIP Heisenberg, "Triumph"; SEP Uncertainty
- The two argued so hard that "Heisenberg reportedly burst into tears at one point." He was 25. — AIP Heisenberg, "Triumph"
- The first exact mathematical form came from E. H. Kennard in 1927: the spread in position times the spread in momentum is always at least ħ/2 (ħ is Planck's constant divided by 2π). — SEP Uncertainty
- **The key point for the script:** in Kennard's form, the "spreads" belong to the quantum state itself. SEP: "there is no reference to simultaneous or joint measurements, nor to any notion of accuracy like the resolving power of the measurement instrument." So it is not about clumsy tools. — SEP Uncertainty
- AIP: "These uncertainties or imprecisions in the measurements were not the fault of the experimenter, said Heisenberg, they were inherent in quantum mechanics." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Uncertainty Principle"
- Heisenberg himself did not like the word "principle." He preferred "inaccuracy relations" (*Ungenauigkeitsrelationen*) or "indeterminacy relations" (*Unbestimmtheitsrelationen*). The English name "uncertainty principle" was used by Condon and Robertson in 1929. — SEP Uncertainty
- Heisenberg won the Nobel Prize in Physics for 1932 "for the creation of quantum mechanics…" The prize was held over a year and given to him in 1933, on 11 December. — NobelPrize.org: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1932/summary/ ; AIP Heisenberg, brief chronology
- Heisenberg later wrote to Born and Jordan that he was sorry the three of them had not shared it. — AIP (Helgoland article)

## 7. The Wave Equation (Schrödinger 1926, Born's probability 1926)

- In late 1925, in Zürich, Peter Debye told Schrödinger (after a talk on de Broglie's waves) that "this way of talking was rather childish… to deal properly with waves, one had to have a wave equation." A few weeks later Schrödinger began a talk: "My colleague Debye suggested that one should have a wave equation; well, I have found one!" — Felix Bloch's recollection (*Physics Today*, 1976), quoted in Straumann, arXiv:quant-ph/0110097: https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/0110097 **UNCERTAIN (a recollection written 50 years later)**
- Schrödinger spent the Christmas holidays of 1925 in the Swiss mountain resort of Arosa. He told a colleague he had barely gone skiing because he was busy with "a few calculations." — University of Zürich News (2017): https://www.news.uzh.ch/en/articles/2017/Schroedinger.html
- A letter to Wilhelm Wien dated 27 December 1925, from Arosa, shows he was working on the hydrogen atom as a wave problem. — Straumann (above)
- The house: UZH names Villa Frisia; Straumann and other sources say Villa Herwig. — UZH; Straumann **UNCERTAIN (house name)**
- He published four papers titled "Quantisierung als Eigenwertproblem" ("Quantization as an Eigenvalue Problem") in *Annalen der Physik* in 1926 (vols. 79, 80, 81). — Wiley (Annalen): https://onlinelibrary.wiley.com/doi/10.1002/andp.19263851302 ; bibliographic details via search results
- Nobel summary of his work: "in 1926 Erwin Schrödinger formulated a wave equation that accurately calculated the energy levels of electrons in atoms." — NobelPrize.org: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1933/schrodinger/facts/
- A gentle image from AIP: "Just as a piano string has a fixed tone, so an electron-wave would have a fixed quantum of energy." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Uncertainty Principle"
- Physicists liked the wave version better because the maths was familiar. Schrödinger said Heisenberg's matrices left him "discouraged, not to say repelled." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Uncertainty Principle"
- Heisenberg's private reply, to Pauli: "The more I think about the physical portion of Schrödinger's theory, the more repulsive I find it… in other words it's crap." — AIP Heisenberg, "The Uncertainty Principle"
- In 1926 Schrödinger showed that matrix mechanics and wave mechanics give the same results. They are the same theory in two languages. — AIP Heisenberg, "The Uncertainty Principle" (AIP says May 1926; the Helgoland article notes that historians now see both his proof and Eckart's as incomplete)
- Max Born gave the wave its meaning: the wave does not say where the electron *is*, but how likely it is to be found there. His Nobel was "for his fundamental research in quantum mechanics, especially for his statistical interpretation of the wavefunction." — NobelPrize.org: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1954/summary/
- The rule first appeared in a note added at the proof stage of Born's 1926 paper "Zur Quantenmechanik der Stoßvorgänge" (*Z. Phys.* 37, 863–867): "More careful consideration shows that the probability is proportional to the square of Ψ." — Neumaier, "The Born rule – 100 years ago and today," arXiv:2502.08545: https://arxiv.org/abs/2502.08545 **UNCERTAIN (exact footnote wording seen via search summary, not the scanned original)**
- Born dated this to June 1926 (the version of 25 June 1926). The name "Born rule" appeared only later, apparently first in 1934. — Neumaier (above)
- Born said the idea came from Einstein, who had tried "interpreting the square of the optical wave amplitudes as probability density for the occurrence of photons. This concept could at once be carried over to the ψ-function." — Born, Nobel Lecture (1954, above)
- Born's Nobel came in 1954, about 28 years after the 1926 paper, shared with Walther Bothe. — NobelPrize.org (1954, above)
- Schrödinger and Paul Dirac shared the 1933 Nobel Prize "for the discovery of new productive forms of atomic theory." — NobelPrize.org: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1933/summary/
- In 1928 Dirac wrote a quantum equation that obeys Einstein's special relativity. It pointed to a particle like the electron but with positive charge: the positron, later found in experiments. — NobelPrize.org: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1933/dirac/facts/

## 8. The Great Argument (Solvay 1927 and 1930, Bohr vs Einstein, interpretations)

- Einstein to Born, 4 December 1926: "The theory yields a lot, but it hardly brings us any closer to the secret of the Old One. In any case I am convinced that He does not throw dice." — AIP Heisenberg, "Triumph": https://history.aip.org/exhibits/heisenberg/triumph.html
- The fifth Solvay conference, on "Électrons et photons," was held in Brussels from 24 to 29 October 1927, chaired by Hendrik Lorentz. Participants stayed at the Hôtel Britannique. — Solvay Institutes archive: https://solvayinstitutes.be/home/archives/conferences-archives/ ; BV, §1.5
- "17 of the 29 attendees photographed were or became Nobel Prize winners – including Curie, who alone among them, had won Nobel Prizes in two separate scientific disciplines." — CERN Courier: https://cerncourier.com/a/all-change/
- Marie Curie was the only woman in the photograph. (Physics 1903, Chemistry 1911.) — CERN Courier (above) **UNCERTAIN ("only woman" confirmed only by popular sources; the photo shows one woman)**
- Born and Heisenberg told the conference: "We regard quantum mechanics as a complete theory for which the fundamental physical and mathematical hypotheses are no longer susceptible of modification." — AIP Heisenberg, "Triumph"
- The famous duel was mostly *not* in the formal sessions. In the printed proceedings "both Bohr and Einstein are in fact relatively silent." The exchanges were "informal discussions… (mainly over breakfast and dinner)," overheard by a few, mainly Heisenberg and Ehrenfest. — BV, preface/§1
- Heisenberg remembered: "We generally met already at breakfast in the hotel, and Einstein began to describe an ideal experiment…", then argued it out on the walk to the conference building. — Heisenberg (1967), quoted in BV
- De Broglie's pilot-wave theory was presented and discussed at length in 1927, contrary to the old story that it was simply ignored. — BV, §2 and preface
- During the general discussion, Paul Ehrenfest wrote the Tower of Babel verse on the blackboard: "…and there confound their language, that they may not understand one another's speech." — BV, opening
- Paul Langevin later said Solvay 1927 was where "the confusion of ideas reached its peak." — BV, opening
- Bohr's own account of the dice line: "Einstein mockingly asked us whether we could really believe that the providential authorities took recourse to dice-playing ('… ob der liebe Gott würfelt'), to which I replied by pointing at the great caution, already called for by ancient thinkers, in ascribing attributes to Providence in every-day language." — Bohr 1949
- Bohr also remembered "a most humorous spirit animated the discussions." — Bohr 1949
- At the sixth Solvay conference in 1930 (on magnetism), Einstein proposed a box full of light, with a clock-driven shutter that lets out one photon at an exactly known moment. By weighing the box before and after (using E = mc²), you would also know the photon's energy exactly, beating the uncertainty rule. — Bohr 1949; Solvay archive (1930 theme)
- Bohr's answer used Einstein's own general relativity: a clock that moves in a gravitational field changes its rate ("the red-shift of the lines in the sun's spectrum"). Weighing the box moves it, which blurs the clock time just enough. — Bohr 1949
- Bohr: "At the outcome of the discussion, to which Einstein himself contributed effectively, it became clear, however, that this argument could not be upheld." — Bohr 1949
- Einstein never fully accepted the Copenhagen view. He believed "nature exists independently of the experimenter," and saw quantum mechanics as incomplete. — AIP Heisenberg, "Triumph"
- **Copenhagen:** "the first general attempt to understand the world of atoms as this is represented by quantum mechanics," mainly from Bohr, with Heisenberg, Born and others. — SEP, "Copenhagen Interpretation": https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-copenhagen/
- Bohr and Heisenberg "never totally agreed," and neither used "the Copenhagen interpretation" as a joint name. The term seems to appear first in Heisenberg (1955). — SEP Copenhagen
- **Many-worlds:** going back to Hugh Everett (1957). "Every time a quantum experiment with different possible outcomes is performed, all outcomes are obtained, each in a different, newly created world." — SEP, "Many-Worlds Interpretation": https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-manyworlds/
- **Pilot wave (Bohmian mechanics):** "discovered by Louis de Broglie in 1927 and rediscovered by David Bohm in 1952." Particles always have real positions, and a wave guides them, "a deterministic motion choreographed by the wave function." — SEP, "Bohmian Mechanics": https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/qm-bohm/
- Physicists still disagree about which picture is right. All of them predict the same experimental results for ordinary experiments. — SEP entries above (neutral summary) **UNCERTAIN ("same predictions" holds for many-worlds and Bohm vs. standard QM; collapse theories differ slightly. ## 9. The Cat in the Box (1935, superposition, decoherence)

- In summer 1935, after the EPR paper, Einstein and Schrödinger wrote to each other. Einstein offered a pile of gunpowder that might or might not explode within a year. Its ψ-function would then mix exploded and unexploded, which he found absurd. — Robert P. Crease, Physics World: https://physicsworld.com/a/the-cat-that-never-dies/
- Einstein's letters led Schrödinger to write "Die gegenwärtige Situation in der Quantenmechanik" ("The present situation in quantum mechanics"), *Naturwissenschaften* 23 (1935), which ran in three parts (pp. 807–812, 823–828, 844–849; NASA ADS: https://ui.adsabs.harvard.edu/abs/1935NW.....23..807S/abstract). "This was the first appearance of Schrödinger's cat." — Physics World (Crease, above); Sudbery, arXiv:2309.06387: https://arxiv.org/abs/2309.06387
- The cat passage (translation by John D. Trimmer, 1980): "One can even set up quite ridiculous cases. A cat is penned up in a steel chamber, along with the following diabolical device (which must be secured against direct interference by the cat): in a Geiger counter there is a tiny bit of radioactive substance, so small, that perhaps in the course of one hour one of the atoms decays, but also, with equal probability, perhaps none; if it happens, the counter tube discharges and through a relay releases a hammer which shatters a small flask of hydrocyanic acid." — quoted in Sudbery, arXiv:2309.06387
- It continues: "The ψ-function of the entire system would express this by having in it the living and the dead cat (pardon the expression) mixed or smeared out in equal parts." — same
- Trimmer's translation was published in *Proceedings of the American Philosophical Society* 124 (1980), 323–338. — Sudbery (above); bibliographic details via search results
- The cat was a criticism, not a recipe. Schrödinger said blurring is harmless inside the nucleus, but "serious misgivings arise if one notices that the uncertainty affects macroscopically tangible and visible things, for which the term 'blurring' seems simply wrong." — Physics World (Crease, above)
- Historian Stephen Brush said the cat "captures the spirit of Einstein's critique better than the published EPR paper." — Physics World (Crease, above)
- **Superposition:** in modern notation, if a cat can be in |alive⟩ and in |dead⟩, the maths also allows |alive⟩ + |dead⟩. — Sudbery (above)
- **Decoherence:** "the study of such situations" where interference is "artificially or spontaneously suppressed." — SEP Decoherence
- The modern theory of decoherence was founded by H. Dieter Zeh in the early 1970s (1970, 1973), with key papers by Wojciech Zurek in the early 1980s. — SEP Decoherence
- The picture: the environment is constantly "measuring" a big object. A cat "has interacted with so many stray particles: photons, air molecules, dust," and each one carries away information. — SEP Decoherence
- How fast: for a speck of dust 10⁻⁵ cm across floating in air, interference is suppressed down to 10⁻¹³ cm, and "suppression of interference on a length scale of 10⁻¹² cm is achieved already after a nanosecond." — SEP Decoherence
- What decoherence does not do: "while decoherence does explain why we do not observe superpositions of measurement results, it does not explain why we do observe measurement results in the first place." — SEP Decoherence
- Serge Haroche and David Wineland won the 2012 Nobel Prize for methods to measure and control single quantum systems. The committee noted that single particles "lose their mysterious quantum properties as soon as they interact with the outside world." — NobelPrize.org: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2012/press-release/
## 10. Spooky Action (EPR 1935, Bell 1964, Clauser 1972, Aspect 1981–82, Nobel 2022)

- A. Einstein, B. Podolsky and N. Rosen, "Can Quantum-Mechanical Description of Physical Reality Be Considered Complete?" *Physical Review* 47, 777. Received 25 March 1935, published 15 May 1935. — APS: https://journals.aps.org/pr/abstract/10.1103/PhysRev.47.777
- Podolsky and Rosen were Einstein's two postdoctoral research associates at the Institute for Advanced Study. — SEP EPR
- The abstract's conclusion: "One is thus led to conclude that the description of reality as given by a wave function is not complete." — APS abstract (above)
- EPR's test for what is "real": "If, without in any way disturbing a system, we can predict with certainty (i.e. with probability equal to unity) the value of a physical quantity, then there exists an element of physical reality corresponding to this physical quantity." — EPR 1935, quoted in Nobel 2022 background
- Einstein was not happy with how the paper came out. He wrote to Schrödinger on 19 June 1935: "For reasons of language this [paper] was written by Podolsky after several discussions. Still, it did not come out as well as I had originally wanted; rather, the essential thing was, so to speak, smothered by formalism [Gelehrsamkeit]." — SEP EPR (citing Fine 1996, p. 35)
- Simple picture of the EPR worry: two particles meet, interact, then fly far apart. Measure one, and you can predict the matching result on the other with certainty, without touching it. Einstein thought this meant each particle must have carried its answer all along, so quantum mechanics was leaving something out. — SEP Entanglement; Nobel 2022 popular
- These missing, secret instructions are called "hidden variables" (or "local hidden variables"). — Nobel 2022 popular
- The Nobel Committee's gentle picture: a machine throws balls of opposite colours in opposite directions. Catch a black one and you know your friend caught a white one. The question is whether each ball was secretly black or white all along, or whether both were "grey" until someone looked. — Nobel 2022 popular
- Quantum mechanics says the balls "have no determined states until they are measured. It is as if both the balls are grey, right up until someone looks at one of them." — Nobel 2022 popular
- Schrödinger gave the idea its name in 1935. In his paper in the *Proceedings of the Cambridge Philosophical Society* he wrote that when two systems interact and separate, "they can no longer be described in the same way as before… I would not call that one but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics, the one that enforces its entire departure from classical lines of thought. By the interaction the two representatives [the quantum states] have become entangled." — SEP Entanglement (quoting Schrödinger 1935, p. 555)
- The Nobel Committee sums it up: "Albert Einstein talked about spooky action at a distance and Erwin Schrödinger said it was quantum mechanics' most important trait." — Nobel 2022 popular
- "Spooky action at a distance" is Einstein's phrase *spukhafte Fernwirkung*, in a letter to Max Born dated 3 March 1947 (*The Born–Einstein Letters*, tr. Irene Born, 1971, p. 155). — Jeremy Bernstein, "Einstein's Entanglements," *Inference*: https://inference-review.com/article/einsteins-entanglements (the letter itself was not read) **UNCERTAIN (letter not read; date and page from a secondary essay)**
- John Stewart Bell, a Northern Irish physicist (1928–1990) who worked at CERN, found a kind of experiment that could decide the question. — Nobel 2022 popular
- The paper: J. S. Bell, "On the Einstein Podolsky Rosen paradox," *Physics Physique Физика* 1, 195 (1964), published 4 November 1964. — APS Bell; Nobel 2022 background (ref. 8)
- It was sent to the journal after Bell's year of leave from CERN, spent at Stanford University, the University of Wisconsin–Madison and Brandeis University. — CERN Scientific Information Service archive guide: https://sis.web.cern.ch/archives/CERN_archive/guide/theory/isabell
- APS calls it "the most important quantum theory paper in 30 years," yet "virtually no one noticed." It gathered fewer than a dozen citations in its first six years. — APS Bell
- The journal was tiny and short-lived. *Physics Physique Физика* existed only from 1964 to 1968 and was set up by Bell Labs physicists Bernd Matthias and Philip Anderson. It even paid authors 10 cents a word. — Physics World (Iulia Georgescu): https://physicsworld.com/a/john-bell-and-the-most-obscure-journal-in-physics/
- Anderson later recalled: "One day a paper came in from a guy neither of us had heard of – John Bell… I accepted it with the hope that its publication would spell the death of Bohmian trajectory quantum mechanics. It's a very famous paper today." — Physics World (quote supplied by Anderson's biographer Andrew Zangwill)
- What Bell's inequality says, in plain words: if the particles really carry hidden instructions, then when you repeat the experiment many times the match-up between the two sides can only reach a certain limit. Quantum mechanics predicts a stronger match-up than that limit allows. — Nobel 2022 popular
- The Nobel Committee: Bell "showed mathematically that no theory based on local hidden variables would be able to reproduce all the results of quantum mechanics." — Nobel 2022 background
- Bell "transformed philosophy into empirical science, forever changing the field." — Nobel 2022 background (Conclusions)
- In 1969 Clauser, Horne, Shimony and Holt ("CHSH") proposed a version of Bell's test that could actually be done in a laboratory. — Nobel 2022 background; Clauser Nobel interview: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2022/clauser/interview/
- In 1972 John Clauser and doctoral student Stuart Freedman (1944–2012) at Berkeley showed "a clear violation of a Bell inequality" that "agreed with the predictions of quantum mechanics." — Nobel 2022 popular
- Their light came from calcium atoms that give off two photons at once. They measured polarisation, the property that polarising sunglasses use. — Nobel 2022 popular
- They rebuilt an older Berkeley apparatus (Carl Kocher's, 1967). It took two years to rebuild and about 200 hours to record the data. — Nobel 2022 background
- The 1972 result beat the Bell limit by about six standard deviations. — Nobel 2022 background
- Alain Aspect, working at the Institut d'Optique in Orsay near Paris with Philippe Grangier, Gérard Roger and Jean Dalibard, ran three experiments published in 1981 and 1982 (*Phys. Rev. Lett.* 47, 460; 49, 91; 49, 1804). — Nobel 2022 background; Freire Junior & others, arXiv:2212.05535: https://arxiv.org/pdf/2212.05535
- His second experiment showed the largest violation of the time, "tens of standard deviations." — Nobel 2022 background
- The famous third one (December 1982, "time-varying analyzers") changed the measurement settings while the photons were already in flight. — Nobel 2022 background; APS: https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.49.1804
- The filters were 6 metres from the source, so the switch had to happen in about 20 nanoseconds ("a few billionths of a second"). That way no message could pass between the two sides in time, even at light speed. — Nobel 2022 background; Nobel 2022 popular
- The Nobel Committee's verdict on Aspect: he "closed an important loophole and provided a very clear result: quantum mechanics is correct and there are no hidden variables." — Nobel 2022 popular
- Aspect's switches were not truly random, so the test was not yet perfect. More than 15 years later Anton Zeilinger's group repeated it under strict conditions, with the two observers 400 metres apart. — Nobel 2022 background
- In 2015–17, four groups (one led by Zeilinger) finally closed the two main loopholes at the same time. — Nobel 2022 background
- One of these, from Delft, used electron spins 1.3 kilometres apart (*Nature*, published 21 October 2015). — Hensen et al.: https://www.nature.com/articles/nature15759
- Zeilinger later used light "from distant galaxies" to choose the filter settings. — Nobel 2022 popular
- **Entanglement cannot send messages faster than light.** Aspect, on the day of his Nobel: "Of course I know that nonlocality does not allow you to send a useful message faster than light." — Aspect Nobel interview: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2022/aspect/interview/
- Why: each person's own results look completely random. The pattern only appears when the two lists are compared afterwards, and comparing them needs an ordinary signal. — IBM on entanglement ("individually random, are tightly correlated"): https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/quantum-computing ; SEP on Bell's theorem (the de Broglie–Bohm theory "does not permit superluminal signalling"): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/bell-theorem/
- Quantum teleportation moves an unknown quantum state from one particle to another, and the original is destroyed in the process. It was "first conducted in 1997 by Anton Zeilinger and his colleagues." — Nobel 2022 popular; Bouwmeester et al., "Experimental quantum teleportation," *Nature* 390 (published 1 Dec 1997): https://www.nature.com/articles/37539
- Zeilinger: teleportation in quantum physics is not like Star Trek. "It's a transfer of information." — Zeilinger Nobel interview: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2022/zeilinger/interview/
- In 1998 Zeilinger's group showed "entanglement swapping," in which two particles that never met become entangled. — Nobel 2022 popular
- China's Micius satellite (launched 2016) shared entangled photon pairs between two places on Earth 1,203 km apart. — Nobel 2022 background
- The 2022 Nobel Prize in Physics went to Alain Aspect, John F. Clauser and Anton Zeilinger "for experiments with entangled photons, establishing the violation of Bell inequalities and pioneering quantum information science." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2022/press-release/
- Aspect was born in 1947 in Agen, France, Clauser in 1942 in Pasadena, and Zeilinger in 1945 in Ried im Innkreis, Austria. — Nobel 2022 popular
- The Committee's closing line: "The first quantum revolution gave us transistors and lasers, but we are now entering a new era thanks to contemporary tools for manipulating systems of entangled particles." — Nobel 2022 popular

## 11. Why You Don't Fall Through the Floor (spin, Pauli, tunnelling)

- In February 1922 in Frankfurt, Otto Stern and Walther Gerlach sent a beam of silver atoms through a magnetic field and saw it split cleanly in two. Classical physics expected one smeared-out band. — Stern–Gerlach PT
- Gerlach's postcard to Niels Bohr, dated 8 February 1922, showed the split beam: "Attached [is] the experimental proof of directional quantization. We congratulate [you] on the confirmation of your theory." — Stern–Gerlach PT
- Gerlach's telegram to Stern said simply: "Bohr is right after all." — Stern–Gerlach PT
- Stern and Gerlach did not yet know they had seen spin. The earliest explanation of the split as electron spin that Friedrich and Herschbach found dates from 1927. — Stern–Gerlach PT
- Electron spin was proposed by two young Leiden students, George Uhlenbeck and Samuel Goudsmit. Their note in *Naturwissenschaften* is dated Saturday 17 October 1925. — Leiden University (Instituut-Lorentz): https://lorentz.leidenuniv.nl/history/spin/spin.html
- They suggested each electron carries an angular momentum of one half (in units of Planck's constant) and a small magnetic moment. — Leiden (above)
- Ralph Kronig had the idea of electron spin several months earlier but did not publish, after a discouraging response from Pauli. — Leiden (above)
- "Spin" is a name, not a literal spinning ball. The 1945 Nobel presentation speech said the electron "behaves to some extent as if it were rapidly rotating." Pauli, in his Nobel lecture, calls spin "an essentially quantum-mechanical property of the electron." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1945/ceremony-speech/ ; Pauli lecture
- Before spin was named, Pauli had described the electron's extra property as a "two-valuedness not describable classically." — Pauli lecture
- Pauli first set out the exclusion principle in Hamburg in spring 1925 (*Z. Physik* 31, 765). — Pauli lecture
- Plain wording from the 1945 Nobel ceremony: "there cannot be more than one electron in each energy state when this state is completely defined." It takes four quantum numbers to define that state completely. — 1945 ceremony speech (above)
- The exclusion principle is why electrons stack up in shells, and so why the chemical elements behave so differently from each other. The presentation speech calls it "essential for the elucidation of the characteristic physical and chemical properties of different elements." — 1945 ceremony speech
- It applies to protons and neutrons too, not only to electrons. — 1945 ceremony speech
- Pauli won the 1945 Nobel Prize in Physics "for the discovery of the Exclusion Principle, also called the Pauli Principle." He was born 25 April 1900 in Vienna and died 15 December 1958 in Zurich. — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1945/pauli/facts/
- Pauli could not be in Stockholm, so his prize was handed to the chargé d'affaires of the US Legation. — 1945 ceremony speech
- **Why solid things feel solid (worded carefully):** in 1967 Freeman Dyson and Andrew Lenard proved that the stability of matter comes from the Pauli exclusion principle. — CERN Courier: https://cerncourier.com/a/putting-the-pauli-exclusion-principle-on-trial/
- Without the exclusion principle, all atoms and bulk matter "would collapse into a condensed high-density phase," releasing an enormous amount of energy (Dyson & Lenard, *J. Math. Phys.* 8, 423, 1967; 9, 698, 1968). — Philip Ball, *Chemistry World*: https://www.chemistryworld.com/opinion/why-is-matter-stable/4013146.article
- Paul Ehrenfest's question was "why are atoms so big?", and his answer was "only the Pauli principle." — Chemistry World (above)
- Honest caveat for narration: how much of a floor's "push back" is electric repulsion and how much is exclusion is still hard to explain simply. Ball writes that there isn't "any obvious path" from the textbook rule to "not being able to occupy the same general region of space." Safe wording: "without the exclusion principle, matter would collapse." — Chemistry World (above)
- CERN Courier's everyday image: "If we tightly grasp a stone in our hands, we neither expect it to vanish nor leak through our flesh and bones." — CERN Courier (above)
- **Tunnelling:** a quantum particle can sometimes turn up on the far side of an energy barrier that, by classical rules, it does not have enough energy to climb. The Nobel committee's image: it makes its way "through a potential mountain." — Nobel 1986 press release: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1986/press-release/
- The first big success of tunnelling was radioactive alpha decay. George Gamow submitted his explanation on 29 July 1928. Ronald Gurney and Edward Condon independently submitted theirs the next day. — E. Merzbacher, "The Early History of Quantum Tunneling," *Physics Today* (2002): https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/the-early-history-of-quantum-tunneling
- The puzzle it solved: alpha particles of about 4.2 MeV were escaping from behind a barrier more than twice as high. — Physics Today (Merzbacher)
- Gurney and Condon's lovely line: "Much has been written of the explosive violence with which the α-particle is hurled from its place in the nucleus. But from the process pictured above, one would rather say that the α-particle slips away almost unnoticed." — Physics Today (Merzbacher, quoting *Nature* 1928)
- The Sun's core is about 15 million °C (27 million °F). — NASA: https://science.nasa.gov/sun/facts/
- Protons repel each other. At the Sun's core temperature they do not have enough heat energy to get over that electric barrier by classical rules. Quantum tunnelling, together with the few fastest protons, lets them fuse anyway. — Case Western Reserve Univ. astronomy notes, "Overcoming the Coulomb Barrier": https://burro.cwru.edu/academics/Astr221/StarPhys/coulomb.html **UNCERTAIN (wording only)**: the popular line "without tunnelling the Sun would not shine" is a fair summary, but it was not found in a NASA or Nobel source.
- The scanning tunnelling microscope, built by Gerd Binnig and Heinrich Rohrer at IBM's research lab in Zurich, won half of the 1986 Physics Nobel "for their design of the scanning tunneling microscope." — Nobel 1986 press release
- It works like reading braille. A fine tip passes about 10 Å (1–2 atom widths) above a surface, and a tunnelling current flows across the gap. — Nobel 1986 press release
- To keep it still, the first version floated on a magnet over a dish of superconducting lead. — Nobel 1986 press release
- The 1973 Physics Nobel went in part to Leo Esaki and Ivar Giaever "for their experimental discoveries regarding tunneling phenomena in semiconductors and superconductors, respectively." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1973/summary/
- Flash memory (USB sticks, phone storage) stores bits as charge trapped on a "floating gate." The first floating-gate memory (D. Kahng & S. M. Sze, Bell Labs, 1967) explained the charging and discharging of that gate by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling through a thin glass-like oxide. — 3D InCites, "The Invention of Tunneling-Based Flash Memory": https://www.3dincites.com/2018/02/invention-tunneling-based-flash-memory/ **UNCERTAIN (trade-press source; modern flash also uses hot-electron injection for writing, so say "tunnelling helps write and erase")**

- **The newest tunnelling Nobel:** the 2025 Nobel Prize in Physics went to John Clarke, Michel H. Devoret and John M. Martinis "for the discovery of macroscopic quantum mechanical tunnelling and energy quantisation in an electric circuit." It was announced on 7 October 2025. — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2025/press-release/
- In 1984 and 1985 they built a superconducting circuit with a Josephson junction (two superconductors separated by a thin non-conducting layer). Together, the charged particles in it behaved "as if they were a single particle that filled the entire circuit," and that whole circuit tunnelled out of its trapped state, "in a system big enough to be held in the hand." — Nobel 2025 press release (above)
- The committee says the prize "provided opportunities for developing the next generation of quantum technology, including quantum cryptography, quantum computers, and quantum sensors." (Superconducting circuits of this kind are the qubits in Google's chips; Devoret is listed with Google Quantum AI.) — Nobel 2025 press release (above)

## 12. The Quantum in Your Pocket (everyday technology)

- Big picture: the Nobel Committee says "The first quantum revolution gave us transistors and lasers." — Nobel 2022 popular
- **Lasers.** In 1917, in "Zur Quantentheorie der Strahlung" ("On the Quantum Theory of Radiation," *Physikalische Zeitschrift* 18), Einstein described stimulated emission. A photon can nudge an already excited atom into giving off a second photon, a matching twin travelling in the same direction. — RP Photonics, "Einstein and the Laser": https://www.rp-photonics.com/spotlight_2008_05_25.html
- It is "rather far-fetched to call Einstein the inventor of the laser," but he found its key physical basis. — RP Photonics (above)
- The first working laser shone on 16 May 1960 at Hughes Research Laboratories in Malibu, built by Theodore Maiman with Irnee D'Haenens and Charles Asawa. — HRL Laboratories: https://www.hrl.com/about/laser
- Its heart was a 1 × 2 cm piece of pink synthetic ruby with silvered ends, inside a coiled photographic flash lamp. — HRL (above)
- The famous "first laser" press photo actually shows a larger ruby and lamp. The photographer found Maiman's real laser too small. — HRL (above)
- The 1964 Physics Nobel (Townes; Basov and Prokhorov) was "for fundamental work in the field of quantum electronics, which has led to the construction of oscillators and amplifiers based on the maser-laser principle." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1964/summary/
- **Transistors.** On 16 December 1947 at Bell Labs, John Bardeen and Walter Brattain made the first working semiconductor amplifier: two gold contacts held by a plastic wedge on a slab of germanium. — Computer History Museum: https://www.computerhistory.org/siliconengine/invention-of-the-point-contact-transistor/
- It amplified signals up to 100 times. They showed it to lab bosses on 23 December, which William Shockley called "a magnificent Christmas present." — CHM (above)
- The name "transistor" came from engineer John Pierce, and Bell Labs announced it on 30 June 1948. — CHM (above)
- Bardeen, Brattain and Shockley shared the 1956 Physics Nobel "for their researches on semiconductors and their discovery of the transistor effect." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1956/summary/
- **LEDs.** In an LED "electricity is directly converted into light particles, photons," so less energy is lost as heat. — Nobel 2014 popular information: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2014/popular-information/
- The 2014 Physics Nobel went to Isamu Akasaki, Hiroshi Amano and Shuji Nakamura "for the invention of efficient blue light-emitting diodes which has enabled bright and energy-saving white light sources." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2014/summary/
- "Incandescent light bulbs had lit the 20th century; the 21st century will be lit by LED lamps." — Nobel 2014 popular
- Numbers from the Nobel page: LEDs had reached just over 300 lumens per watt, against 16 for ordinary bulbs and about 70 for fluorescent lamps. About one fourth of the world's electricity goes on lighting. Old bulbs last about 1,000 hours and LEDs up to 100,000. — Nobel 2014 popular (figures as of 2014)
- **MRI.** Atomic nuclei in a strong magnetic field absorb radio waves at a matching frequency ("resonance") and give radio waves back as they return. — Nobel 2003 Medicine press release: https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2003/press-release/
- MRI mostly reads the nuclei of hydrogen atoms, in water. Water is about two thirds of body weight. — Nobel 2003 press release
- The same line of work earned three Nobels. Rabi won in 1944 "for his resonance method for recording the magnetic properties of atomic nuclei." Bloch and Purcell won in 1952 for "new methods for nuclear magnetic precision measurements." Lauterbur and Mansfield won the 2003 Medicine prize "for their discoveries concerning magnetic resonance imaging." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1944/summary/ ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1952/summary/ ; https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/medicine/2003/summary/
- In 2003 the Nobel Assembly said more than 60 million MRI examinations were done worldwide each year. — Nobel 2003 press release
- The 1922 Stern–Gerlach plaque in Frankfurt calls the experiment "the basis of important scientific and technological developments in the 20th century, such as nuclear magnetic resonance, atomic clocks, or lasers." — Stern–Gerlach PT
- **Atomic clocks.** The second is defined using caesium atoms. "Atomic clocks count 9,192,631,770 of those microwave oscillations, and we call the elapsed time interval a second." — NIST: https://www.nist.gov/si-redefinition/second
- Commercial caesium clocks keep time to within 1/3,000,000 of a second per year. — NIST (above)
- The atoms' ticking is a quantum jump. The outer electron flips to a higher energy state when microwaves hit exactly the right frequency. — NIST, "Clocks Galore": https://www.nist.gov/atomic-clocks/how-atomic-clocks-work/clocks-galore
- GPS satellites carry atomic clocks. Early GPS used both caesium and rubidium clocks, and "Today, the satellites carry only rubidium clocks." — NIST, "Clocks Galore"
- Why it matters: "if the system's time were off by even 100 nanoseconds, a position estimate would be off by 30 meters, or nearly 100 feet." — NIST, "Clocks Galore"
- Callback to video 1: GPS clocks must also be corrected for relativity (net about 38 µs per day fast). — NIST (see the video 1 fact file, §15): https://www.nist.gov/atomic-clocks/a-powerful-tool-for-science/putting-einstein-test
- The 1989 Physics Nobel went in part to Norman Ramsey "for the invention of the separated oscillatory fields method and its use in the hydrogen maser and other atomic clocks." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1989/summary/

## 13. Machines Made of Maybe (quantum computers) + closing

- A qubit, or quantum bit, "is the basic unit of information used to encode data in quantum computing… the quantum equivalent of the traditional bit." — IBM: https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/qubit
- The word "qubit" is credited to physicist Benjamin Schumacher. — IBM (above)
- Qubits can be made from photons, electrons, trapped ions, superconducting circuits and atoms. — IBM (above)
- When a qubit is measured, "its state collapses from a superposition of possibilities into a binary state… either a zero or a one." — IBM: https://www.ibm.com/think/topics/quantum-computing
- IBM calls interference "the engine of quantum computing." — IBM (above)
- **The common myth, corrected.** Scott Aaronson calls the "both 0 and 1 at the same time… try all possible solutions in parallel" explanation "the fundamental misstep of quantum computing popularization." — Quanta Magazine: https://www.quantamagazine.org/why-is-quantum-computing-so-hard-to-explain-20210608/
- If you simply measure an equal superposition of all answers, "you'll just see and read a random answer." — Aaronson, Quanta (above)
- What a quantum algorithm really does: it must "choreograph a pattern of constructive and destructive interference so that for each wrong answer the contributions to its amplitude cancel each other out, whereas for the right answer the contributions reinforce each other." — Aaronson, Quanta (above)
- In May 1981, at a conference at MIT, Richard Feynman gave the keynote talk "Simulating Physics with Computers." It was published in 1982. — Feynman 1982 (received 7 May 1981) **UNCERTAIN (venue)**: the MIT/Endicott House location comes from secondary accounts; the paper itself does not name the place.
- He proposed: "Let the computer itself be built of quantum mechanical elements which obey quantum mechanical laws." — Feynman 1982
- His famous ending, checked against the printed paper: "nature isn't classical, dammit, and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical, and by golly it's a wonderful problem, because it doesn't look so easy. Thank you." — Feynman 1982
- In 1994 Peter Shor (AT&T) showed that a quantum computer could factor large numbers efficiently. That is a problem "generally thought to be hard on a classical computer," and it is used "as the basis of several proposed cryptosystems." The paper first appeared at FOCS, Santa Fe, 20–22 Nov 1994. — arXiv: https://arxiv.org/abs/quant-ph/9508027
- In 2012 John Preskill of Caltech coined the phrase "quantum supremacy" for the moment a quantum computer beats the best supercomputer at some task. — Quanta: https://www.quantamagazine.org/google-and-ibm-clash-over-quantum-supremacy-claim-20191023/
- Google, "Quantum supremacy using a programmable superconducting processor," *Nature*, published 23 October 2019. — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-019-1666-5
- The Sycamore chip had 54 qubits, but "One qubit did not function properly, so the device uses 53 qubits." — Nature 2019 (above)
- The claim: "Our Sycamore processor takes about 200 seconds to sample one instance of a quantum circuit a million times—our benchmarks currently indicate that the equivalent task for a state-of-the-art classical supercomputer would take approximately 10,000 years." — Nature 2019 abstract
- The qubits are tiny superconducting circuits, cooled until "currents and voltages behave quantum mechanically." — Nature 2019
- IBM pushed back. It argued its Summit supercomputer (the size of two basketball courts, at Oak Ridge) could do the task in 2.5 days, not 10,000 years. — Quanta (2019, above)
- Quanta's fair summary: what matters to computer scientists is that the quantum time grows slowly while the best known classical time grows exponentially, "whether it's 2.5 days or 10,000 years" (William Fefferman). — Quanta (2019)
- Google's Willow chip was announced on 9 December 2024. It has 105 qubits. — Google: https://blog.google/technology/research/google-willow-quantum-chip/
- **Willow claim 1 (error correction, peer-reviewed):** as Google grew its grid of qubits from 3×3 to 5×5 to 7×7, "each time… we were able to cut the error rate in half." Google calls this the first system "below threshold." — Google blog; *Nature*, "Quantum error correction below the surface code threshold," published 9 Dec 2024: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-024-08449-y
- **Willow claim 2 (benchmark, a separate result):** Willow "performed a standard benchmark computation in under five minutes that would take one of today's fastest supercomputers 10 septillion (that is, 10^25) years." — Google blog
- Google's own caveat: that benchmark (random circuit sampling) "has no known real-world applications." — Google blog
- Google also expects classical computers "to keep improving on this benchmark," as happened after 2019. — Google blog
- Closing thought: Zeilinger on the Nobel phone call says that "with very few symbols… you can explain a whole lot of things from the smallest quantum particles up to the origin of the universe." — Zeilinger Nobel interview
- Zeilinger: quantum physics "is absolutely robust against all experimental challenges… On the other hand, the theory is also mathematically extremely beautiful." — Zeilinger Nobel interview
- Aspect, on whether Einstein lost the argument: "There is not one who wins and the other one who loses. Bohr wins from a certain point of view, but Einstein wins because he spotted something extraordinary." — Aspect Nobel interview
- Aspect: "my experiment was trying to find a limit of quantum mechanics, and we didn't find it." — Aspect Nobel interview

## UNCERTAIN — collected

1. **"Nobody understands quantum mechanics" (Feynman):** wording verified from a web transcript of the 1964 Cornell lecture, not the printed book. Fine to use; say "in a 1964 lecture at Cornell."
2. **Planck's "act of desperation" letter (to R. W. Wood, 7 October 1931):** the words are usually given as "an act of desperation… I had to obtain a positive result, under any circumstances and at whatever cost." Kragh (Physics World) quotes the same letter as "a purely formal assumption and I really did not give it much thought except that no matter what the cost, I must bring about a positive result," and uses "act of desperation" as Planck's own phrase. The letter is in the Archive for the History of Quantum Physics. It was not seen directly, and translations differ. Safe: "Planck later called it an act of desperation."
3. **Planck's postcard to Rubens on 7 October 1900:** from Pais (via JQI). Date of the talk: 19 October (Kragh, MPG, Mukunda) vs 17 October (JQI). Use 19 October.
4. **Rutherford's "15-inch shell" quote:** wording is stable; the occasion is given variously (a 1936 Cambridge lecture, the 1925 Cawthron Lecture, Eve's 1939 biography p. 311).
5. **Davisson–Germer "liquid-air bottle exploded":** biographies only. APS: "their equipment broke."
6. **Einstein's letter to Langevin** ("lifted a corner of the great veil"): the phrase is well attested (Bacciagaluppi & Valentini). The exact date and Langevin's "a bit strange" remark are secondary.
7. **De Broglie as duke from 1960:** non-primary pages only.
8. **Millikan's autobiography chapter title:** search summary only.
9. **Kilogram redefinition start date (20 May 2019):** standard, but not on the NIST page we read. The November 2018 vote is verified.
10. **"Einstein and Bohr's prizes announced together":** likely, not confirmed on the pages read.
11. **Who "discovered" the quantum:** Planck (tradition, Nobel) vs Einstein (Kuhn, Darrigol). A real historians' debate. Narrate as "Planck opened the door, perhaps without quite meaning to."
12. **Helgoland story:** from Heisenberg's 1969 memoir. AIP calls it "dubious at best" as an origin myth. The 10-day visit is confirmed, but how much work he did there is not.
13. **Goethe / *West-östlicher Divan* on Helgoland:** memoir via retellings only.
14. **Arosa "mystery woman":** leave out. The Moore (1989) biography's story goes back to rumours Max Delbrück told in the 1970s, published without evidence (Gronau et al. 2026, *Berichte zur Wissenschaftsgeschichte*: https://pmc.ncbi.nlm.nih.gov/articles/PMC13397394/). The Weyl "late erotic outburst" quote rests on Pais's memory. The holiday itself and the 27 Dec 1925 letter are solid.
15. **Arosa house name:** Villa Frisia (UZH) vs Villa Herwig (Straumann, Moore).
16. **"God does not play dice":** the letter wording (4 Dec 1926, to Born) is "I am convinced that He does not throw dice" (AIP); the German is usually given as "…dass der nicht würfelt," with "der Alte" (the Old One). The popular "God does not play dice with the universe" is a paraphrase.
17. **"Einstein, stop telling God what to do":** no primary source found. Bohr's own 1949 account is gentler: he urged "great caution… in ascribing attributes to Providence in every-day language." Use Bohr's version.
18. **Born footnote exact wording:** via secondary quotation (Neumaier 2025); original scan not read.
19. **Debye "childish" / "I have found one!":** Felix Bloch's 1976 recollection.
20. **Tonomura electron rate:** paper says ~10³/s over the whole field; Hitachi page says 10/s. Both agree only one electron at a time.
21. **Young 1803:** a card splitting a beam, not two slits; wording via secondary source.
22. **Taylor 1909 "single photons":** very dim light, not proven single photons.
23. **Curie "only woman":** popular sources and the photo; not stated on the Solvay Institutes page.
24. **Einstein's 1920 "such joy" letter:** via search snippets only.
25. **Dirac unit "one word per hour":** Farmelo via The Conversation; a joke.
26. **"All interpretations give the same predictions":** true for Bohm and many-worlds in ordinary experiments; collapse theories differ in principle. Keep it soft.
27. **"Spooky action at a distance":** the date (3 March 1947) and page come from Bernstein's *Inference* essay. The Born–Einstein letter itself was not read.
28. **Einstein's "Do you really believe the Moon exists only when observed?"** is quoted by APS, but it is a later recollection (usually traced to Abraham Pais). Treat it as "Einstein reportedly asked…".
29. **Where Bell wrote the 1964 paper:** CERN SIS says it was sent "after a year's leave" spent at Stanford, Wisconsin and Brandeis. Physics World says "while working at CERN." Other accounts say it was written at Brandeis. Safe wording: "during a year away from CERN, in America."
30. **Journal citation:** APS and the Nobel committee give *Physics* 1, 195. Physics World prints "(3 195)", which is really issue 3 of volume 1.
31. **Bell's Nobel nomination** (MacTutor): not checked against the Nobel nomination archive.
32. **Aspect–Bell "permanent job" line:** secondary retelling only.
33. **Pauli effect:** folklore (Gamow 1966).
34. **Pauli's "abdication of a king" letter to Born (1955)** about Einstein's Princeton speech: only seen in search snippets. The ETH Library confirms the speech, not the letter wording.
35. **Sun tunnelling:** the physics is verified (CWRU lecture notes + NASA temperature). No NASA/Nobel line was found saying "without tunnelling the Sun wouldn't shine." Figures like "10^28 collisions" or "20 orders of magnitude" came from blogs and are not used.
36. **Flash memory and tunnelling:** trade-press source (3D InCites). Modern NAND erases (and often writes) by Fowler–Nordheim tunnelling, but some flash writes by hot-electron injection. Say "tunnelling moves the charge in and out."
37. **Feynman 1981 venue** (MIT Endicott House): not in the paper itself.
38. **Willow "many parallel universes" line:** the Google blog says the result "lends credence" to the multiverse idea. That is one founder's opinion, not a finding. Leave it out or flag it clearly.
