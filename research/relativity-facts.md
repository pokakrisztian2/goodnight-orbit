# Fact Sheet — "The Confusing Parts of the Theory of Relativity, Explained Gently"

Compiled 2026-09-28. Every line was checked against the listed source with WebSearch/WebFetch (no Wikipedia).
Legend: plain line = verified. **UNCERTAIN** = not fully confirmed, or wording/page varies — soften it in narration or leave it out.
"CPAE" = *The Collected Papers of Albert Einstein* (Princeton University Press). The Princeton online edition now sends document links to a database landing page, so CPAE items are cited by volume and document number.

> Corrections to the brief:
> - Pound–Rebka was *measured* in late 1959 to early 1960. The paper was received 9 March 1960; the proposal was published in 1959.
> - The "1/10,000 of a proton" figure is LIGO's *design sensitivity*. It is not the measured size of GW150914 itself.
> - The Besso line is "Thank you. I've completely solved the problem" in the Stachel/Isaacson translation. Norton's rendering is "Thanks to you, I have completely solved my problem."
> - Hilbert's "race" is historically debated (see Beat 10).

## 1. Galileo's ship (1632)

- Galileo's *Dialogue Concerning the Two Chief World Systems* was published in Florence in 1632. It is a conversation between Salviati, Sagredo and Simplicio. — MacTutor, St Andrews: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Extras/Galileo_Dialogue/
- Salviati (Second Day, modern translation by Stillman Drake): "Shut yourself up with some friend in the main cabin below decks on some large ship, and have with you there some flies, butterflies, and other small flying animals. Have a large bowl of water with some fish in it; hang up a bottle that empties drop by drop into a wide vessel beneath it." — Galileo, *Dialogue*, tr. S. Drake (University of California Press, 1953/1967), pp. 186–187. **UNCERTAIN (page numbers only)**: the pages come from secondary citations; the wording is the standard Drake text.
- The older 1661 Salusbury translation reads: "Shut yourself up with some friend in the largest room below decks of some large ship and there procure gnats, flies, and such other small winged creatures…" — MacTutor (link above)
- Point of the passage: while the ship moves uniformly and steadily (Drake: "so long as the motion is uniform and not fluctuating this way and that" — **UNCERTAIN wording, seen only via secondary copies**), the butterflies, fish and drops behave exactly as when it is at rest. Nothing inside the cabin tells you whether you are moving. This is Galilean relativity. — Drake tr., pp. 186–187; MacTutor

## 2. Newton: absolute space and time (1687)

- The *Principia* was printed in London in 1687, paid for largely by Edmond Halley himself. — Royal Society: https://royalsociety.org/news/2012/The-fishy-blunder-that-nearly-prevented-Newtons-masterpiece-from-being-published/
- **UNCERTAIN:** Halley's letter of 5 July 1687 telling Newton the printing was finished, with 20 copies for Newton. Only seen in secondary summaries (e.g. https://www.historyofinformation.com/detail.php?id=1679).
- The Scholium after the Definitions: "Absolute, true, and mathematical time, of itself, and from its own nature, flows equably without relation to anything external." — Stanford Encyclopedia of Philosophy (Motte/Cajori translation): https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/newton-stm/scholium.html
- Same Scholium: "Absolute space, in its own nature, without relation to anything external, remains always similar and immovable." — SEP, same page
- Newton's rotating-bucket passage, from the same Scholium, argues that true rotation shows itself through the water climbing the sides of the bucket. — SEP, same page

## 3. Light, the aether, and Maxwell (1860s)

- Maxwell worked out the speed of his electromagnetic waves at his Scottish home, Glenlair, in summer 1861. Back in London he compared it with measured values and found it matched the speed of light. — Physics World: https://physicsworld.com/a/james-clerk-maxwell-a-force-for-physics/
- Maxwell (On Physical Lines of Force, *Phil. Mag.* 1861–62, italics his): "We can scarcely avoid the inference that light consists in the transverse undulations of the same medium which is the cause of electric and magnetic phenomena." — Physics World (above)
- "A Dynamical Theory of the Electromagnetic Field" was read to the Royal Society on 8 December 1864 and published in *Phil. Trans.* 155 (1865). — Royal Society: https://royalsocietypublishing.org/rstl/article/doi/10.1098/rstl.1865.0008/118816/VIII-A-dynamical-theory-of-the-electromagnetic
- Maxwell to his cousin Charles Cay, 5 January 1865: "I have also a paper afloat containing an electromagnetic theory of light, which, till I am convinced to the contrary, I hold to be great guns." — Physics World (above)
- In that paper Maxwell compared his value with measured speeds of light: Fizeau 314,858,000 m/s, Foucault 298,000,000 m/s, and 308,000,000 m/s from stellar aberration. — Maxwell 1865 (Phil. Trans.), full text: http://www.bem.fi/library/1865-001.pdf

## 4. Michelson–Morley (1887, Cleveland)

- Albert Michelson (Case School of Applied Science) and Edward Morley (Western Reserve University) worked in Cleveland, Ohio. They reported in November 1887: "On the Relative Motion of the Earth and the Luminiferous Ether," *American Journal of Science* 34, 333–345. — APS News: https://www.aps.org/apsnews/2007/11/november-1887-michelson-uminiferous-ether ; AJS: https://ajsonline.org/article/62505-on-the-relative-motion-of-the-earth-and-the-luminiferous-ether
- Setup: optics on a stone slab about 1.5 m square and 0.3 m thick, floating on mercury in a cast-iron trough so it could turn smoothly. Four mirrors at the corners bounced the light back and forth for a path of about 11 m. — Michelson & Morley 1887, AJS 34 (above)
- Observations were made at noon on 8, 9 and 11 July 1887, and in the evening on 8, 9 and 12 July 1887. — Michelson & Morley 1887
- Expected: a shift of about 0.4 of a fringe from the Earth's orbital motion through the ether. — Michelson & Morley 1887
- Actual: "certainly less than the twentieth part of this, and probably less than the fortieth part." — Michelson & Morley 1887
- Both men went on believing light was a vibration in the ether. — APS News (above)
- Michelson's first try (1881) in a Berlin basement failed because of vibration from street traffic. He moved it to a cellar under the Astrophysical Observatory in Potsdam, where the April 1881 runs gave a null result. — paper "Michelson's first ether-drift experiment in Berlin and Potsdam": https://www.researchgate.net/publication/234868269_Michelson's_first_ether-drift_experiment_in_Berlin_and_Potsdam
- Michelson became the first American to win a Nobel science prize (Physics, 1907). — APS News (above)

## 5. FitzGerald, Lorentz, Poincaré

- George FitzGerald's letter "The ether and the earth's atmosphere," *Science* 13 (1889), 390, is less than half a page long. It proposes that moving bodies shorten along their direction of motion. — MacTutor: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/HistTopics/Special_relativity/ ; Brush, *Isis* 58 (1967) 230–232
- Lorentz, not knowing of FitzGerald, proposed the same contraction in 1892. He wrote down the transformations that now bear his name in 1899 and 1904. — MacTutor (above)
- Poincaré (1898, "La mesure du temps"): "we have no direct intuition about the equality of two time intervals." — MacTutor (above)
- Poincaré presented "Sur la dynamique de l'électron" to the Paris Académie des Sciences on 5 June 1905. The longer version, which showed the Lorentz transformations form a group, was submitted 23 July 1905 (published in Palermo, 1906). — MacTutor (above); arXiv:1710.00706 https://arxiv.org/abs/1710.00706

## 6. Young Einstein (1879–1905)

- Born in Ulm, 14 March 1879, and grew up in Munich. — ETH Library: https://library.ethz.ch/en/collections-and-archives/short-portraits/einstein-albert-1879-1955.html
- Around age 4 or 5 his father showed him a compass. The needle convinced him there had to be "something behind things, something deeply hidden." — AIP exhibit: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early1.htm ; Autobiographical Notes (Schilpp 1949), p. 9
- At 12 a geometry "booklet" gave him "a wonder": "This lucidity and certainty made an indescribable impression on me." — AIP exhibit, early1 (above)
- He hated the Munich gymnasium (the Luitpold Gymnasium). A teacher suggested he leave, and at 15 he quit mid-term, in December 1894, to join his parents in Italy. — AIP exhibit, early1 (above); AIP exhibit home: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/
- In 1895 he failed the entrance exam for the Zurich Polytechnic. He did very well in maths and science but poorly in languages and history. — ETH Library (above); AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early2.htm
- He then spent 1895–96 at the cantonal school in Aarau (from about the end of October 1895). There, at about 16, he imagined chasing a light beam. — AIP/Stachel essay: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/essay-einstein-relativity.htm ; J. Norton (Pittsburgh): https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/papers/Chasing.pdf
- Autobiographical Notes (written 1946, publ. 1949, pp. 52–53): "…a paradox upon which I had already hit at the age of sixteen: If I pursue a beam of light with the velocity c (velocity of light in a vacuum), I should observe such a beam of light as an electromagnetic field at rest though spatially oscillating. There seems to be no such thing, however…" — Norton (above); Schilpp ed., *Albert Einstein: Philosopher-Scientist* (1949)
- In an Aarau essay written in French at 16 he gave, as a reason to study physics, "my lack of imagination and practical talent." — AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early2.htm
- He studied at the Zurich Polytechnic (now ETH) from 1896 to 1900 and earned a teaching diploma in mathematics and science. — ETH Library (above)
- His friend Marcel Grossmann shared his lecture notes. Einstein later wrote: "I would rather not speculate on what would have become of me without these notes." — AIP, early2 (above)
- Mileva Marić (born 19 December 1875, Titel) was the only woman in his physics section. They married on 6 January 1903 in Bern. — AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early4.htm ; Scientific American: https://www.scientificamerican.com/blog/guest-blog/the-forgotten-life-of-einsteins-first-wife/
- Children: Lieserl (born January 1902; her fate is unknown), Hans Albert (14 May 1904) and Eduard (28 July 1910). — Scientific American (above)
- Einstein to Mileva, 27 March 1901: "How happy and proud I will be when the two of us together will have brought our work on relative motion to a victorious conclusion." — Scientific American (above); CPAE vol. 1
- **UNCERTAIN / contested:** how much Mileva contributed. AIP says there is "no solid evidence that she made any significant contribution." Others argue the collaboration cannot be disentangled. Present both views. — AIP early4; Scientific American
- Patent office: he was appointed Technical Expert Class III (on trial) at 3,500 francs a year. His first working day is usually given as 23 June 1902; the Caltech Einstein Papers Project says he "went to work at the Swiss Patent Office" in June 1902 (https://www.einstein.caltech.edu/what-we-do/published-volumes/volume-5); the Swiss IP Institute says "July 1902." He was promoted to Class II on 1 April 1906 and left in autumn 1909. — Swiss Federal Institute of Intellectual Property: https://www.ige.ch/en/about-us/the-history-of-the-ipi/einstein/einstein-at-the-patent-office
- He worked in Room 86 on the third floor, at the corner of Speichergasse and Genfergasse. He called the office "that worldly cloister where I hatched my most beautiful ideas." — IGE (above)
- He called the job "a kind of salvation." — AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early3.htm
- **UNCERTAIN:** that Einstein personally examined patents for clock synchronization. Such patents did pass through the Bern office, and Bern had electrically synchronized city clocks from 1890. The record does not show which files Einstein handled. — Physics Today review of Galison, *Einstein's Clocks, Poincaré's Maps*: https://physicstoday.aip.org/reviews/einsteins-clocks-poincares-maps-empires-of-time
- Olympia Academy: from 1902 Einstein, Maurice Solovine and Conrad Habicht met to read and argue about Spinoza, Hume, Mach, Poincaré and others. The name mocked the official academies. — AIP early3 (above); IGE "Einstein in Bern": https://www.ige.ch/en/about-us/the-history-of-the-ipi/einstein/einstein-in-bern
- He lived at Kramgasse 49 (now the Einstein House) from 1903 to 1905. — IGE "Einstein in Bern"
- Michele Besso walked home from the patent office with him every day. Einstein called him "the best sounding board in Europe." — AIP early3
- Kyoto lecture (14 December 1922): Einstein visited Besso with a problem he could not solve. The next day he told him "Thank you. I've completely solved the problem." (Stachel's translation, also used by Isaacson.) Norton's version is "Thanks to you, I have completely solved my problem." **UNCERTAIN (wording varies by translation)**. — Norton: https://sites.pitt.edu/~jdnorton/teaching/HPS_0410/chapters/origins_pathway/index.html
- In the same lecture: "My solution actually concerned the concept of time… there is an unbreakable connection between time and signal velocity." And: "it took only five weeks to complete what is now known as the special theory of relativity." — Norton (above)

## 7. 1905, the miracle year

- Letter to Habicht (May 1905; CPAE vol. 5, doc. 27) opens "you frozen whale…" and promises four papers. He calls the light paper "very revolutionary" and the relativity paper still "a rough draft." — Isaacson, *Einstein: His Life and Universe* (2007), ch. 1; excerpt: https://abcnews.com/GMA/Books/story?id=3023514&page=1
- The papers, all in *Annalen der Physik*: light quanta / photoelectric effect (received 18 March), dissertation (dated 30 April), Brownian motion (received 11 May), "On the Electrodynamics of Moving Bodies" (received 30 June), E=mc² (received 27 September). — arXiv historical summary: https://arxiv.org/pdf/physics/0701240 ; Stachel ed., *Einstein's Miraculous Year* (Princeton UP, 1998)
- "Zur Elektrodynamik bewegter Körper," *Ann. Phys.* 17, 891–921, received 30 June 1905 and published about three months later. — Physics Today: https://physicstoday.aip.org/news/einsteins-special-relativity-paper-arrives-at-annalen-der-physik
- Postulate 1 (in the paper's words): "the same laws of electrodynamics and optics will be valid for all frames of reference for which the equations of mechanics hold good." — Einstein 1905, Perrett & Jeffery tr. (1923): https://www.fourmilab.ch/etexts/einstein/specrel/specrel.pdf
- Postulate 2: "light is always propagated in empty space with a definite velocity c which is independent of the state of motion of the emitting body." — same
- "The introduction of a 'luminiferous ether' will prove to be superfluous." — same
- On simultaneity: "If, for instance, I say, 'That train arrives here at 7 o'clock,' I mean something like this: 'The pointing of the small hand of my watch to 7 and the arrival of the train are simultaneous events.'" — same, §1
- Time dilation, §4: a clock taken around a closed path and brought back "will be ½tv²/c² second slow", so "a balance-clock at the equator must go more slowly… than a precisely similar clock situated at one of the poles." — same
- Length contraction: in the paper, a moving rigid sphere appears as an ellipsoid, shortened along its direction of motion by the factor √(1−v²/c²). — same, §4
- The only person thanked is Besso: "I have had the loyal assistance of my friend and colleague M. Besso." — same, closing lines
- The E=mc² paper, "Ist die Trägheit eines Körpers von seinem Energieinhalt abhängig?" (*Ann. Phys.* 18, 639–641), is about three pages long. It is signed "Bern, September 1905" and marked "Eingegangen 27. September 1905" (received 27 September 1905). — original scan: http://info.phys.unm.edu/~alandahl/phys262f06/Einstein_Emc2paper1905.pdf ; MIT Libraries: https://libraries.mit.edu/150books/2011/02/20/1905/
- The famous equation never appears as "E=mc²" in the paper. It says: "If a body gives off the energy L in the form of radiation, its mass diminishes by L/c²" (the original uses V for the speed of light). — same scan; translation: https://www.fourmilab.ch/etexts/einstein/E_mc2/www/
- It ends by suggesting a test: "with bodies whose energy-content is variable to a high degree (e.g. with radium salts) the theory may be successfully put to the test." — same

## 8. Minkowski (Cologne, 1908)

- Hermann Minkowski gave "Raum und Zeit" in Cologne on 21 September 1908, at the 80th Assembly of German Natural Scientists and Physicians. — Damour, arXiv:0807.1300 https://arxiv.org/abs/0807.1300 ; arXiv:1210.6929
- Exact English wording (Perrett & Jeffery tr.): "Henceforth space by itself, and time by itself, are doomed to fade away into mere shadows, and only a kind of union of the two will preserve an independent reality." — Damour, p. 11; *The Principle of Relativity* (Dover 1952)
- Also from the lecture: "Three-dimensional geometry becomes a chapter in four-dimensional physics." — Damour, p. 11
- Minkowski called "3·10⁵ km = √−1 secs" a "mystic formula." — Damour, note 3
- Printed in *Physikalische Zeitschrift* 10 (1909) 104–115. Minkowski died of appendicitis on 12 January 1909, aged 44, less than four months after the talk. — Damour, p. 2

## 9. "The happiest thought of my life" (1907)

- In late 1907, at the patent office, while writing a review article on relativity, Einstein realised that a man falling from a roof would not feel his own weight. — Janssen, "No success like failure…" (Cambridge Companion to Einstein, 2014): https://www.physics.umd.edu/grt/taj/675e/QuestforGR.pdf
- The phrase "der glücklichste Gedanke meines Lebens" (the happiest thought of my life) comes from an unpublished 1920 manuscript written for *Nature* (CPAE vol. 7, doc. 31). He told the story again in Kyoto in 1922. — Janssen, note 29
- From that manuscript: "for an observer freely falling from the roof of a house, no gravitational field exists while he is falling, at least not in his immediate surroundings." — Janssen, §2
- The review, requested by Johannes Stark, was "Über das Relativitätsprinzip…," *Jahrbuch der Radioaktivität und Elektronik* 4 (1907). It contains the first statement of the equivalence principle. — arXiv:2209.13781 https://arxiv.org/abs/2209.13781
- **UNCERTAIN:** "November 1907" as the exact month. It is only inferred from his letters; say "late 1907."

## 10. General relativity, 1912–1915

- In 1912 Einstein returned to the ETH Zurich as a professor. His old classmate Marcel Grossmann, by then a mathematics professor there, showed him Riemann's geometry. — Janssen (above); AIP early4
- Einstein reportedly told Grossmann: "You must help me or else I'll go crazy." **UNCERTAIN (reported, not documented in his hand)** — Pais, *Subtle is the Lord* (1982), p. 212; Stachel 2002, p. 107 (both via Janssen)
- The Einstein–Grossmann "Entwurf" ("outline") theory was published in May 1913. — Janssen, §3
- In late August 1913 the "hole argument" convinced Einstein, wrongly, that fully general field equations were impossible. — Janssen, §3; SEP "The Hole Argument": https://plato.stanford.edu/entries/spacetime-holearg/
- He presented papers to the Prussian Academy in Berlin on 4, 11, 18 and 25 November 1915. The 18 November paper explained Mercury, and the 25 November paper gave the final field equations. — Caltech Einstein Papers Project: https://www.einstein.caltech.edu/news/one-hundred-years-of-einsteins-field-equations-and-of-the-explanation-of-mercurys-perihelion
- The theory accounted for about 43 arcseconds per century of Mercury's orbit shift that the pull of other planets could not explain. — Janssen, note 19; arXiv:1411.7370
- About ten days before the final paper he told Hilbert he was suffering from exhaustion and stomach pains. — Caltech (above)
- Hilbert's paper is dated 20 November 1915 but was published on 31 March 1916. His printer's proofs, found in the 1990s, lack the explicit field equations. — Corry, Renn & Stachel, *Science* 278, 1270 (1997): https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.278.5341.1270 . **UNCERTAIN (priority still debated; frame it as a friendly race, not a theft)**
- Pais: the Mercury result gave Einstein "palpitations of the heart" (told to Fokker) — "the strongest emotional experience in Einstein's scientific life." **UNCERTAIN (page ≈ 253 in Pais, ch. 14, not checked directly)**
- To Ehrenfest, 17 January 1916: "For a few days I was beside myself with joyous excitement." — CPAE vol. 8, doc. 182
- To Sommerfeld, 28 November 1915: "…one of the most exciting, exhausting times of my life, indeed also one of the most successful." — CPAE vol. 8, doc. 153

## 11. The 1919 eclipse

- The eclipse was on 29 May 1919. Expeditions went to Sobral in northern Brazil (Andrew Crommelin and Charles Davidson) and to the island of Príncipe (Arthur Eddington with the clockmaker E. T. Cottingham). Frank Dyson, Astronomer Royal, chaired the organising committee. — Kennefick, arXiv:0709.0685 https://arxiv.org/abs/0709.0685
- Both parties sailed from England on 8 March 1919. — Max Planck Society: https://www.mpg.de/9244824/solar-eclipse-1919 ; Royal Observatory Greenwich: https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1289
- Einstein predicted 1.75 arcseconds of starlight bending at the Sun's edge, double the "Newtonian" value of about 0.87. — MPG (above); Lemos, arXiv:1912.05587
- Results: Sobral 4-inch lens 1.98 ± 0.12″; Príncipe 1.61 ± 0.30″. The Sobral main telescope (≈0.93″) was set aside because of focus problems. — Dyson, Eddington & Davidson, *Phil. Trans. R. Soc. A* 220, 291 (1920); Lemos pp. 42–43; Kennefick
- Eddington: the weather on the day was bad. His team took 16 photographs, with "the beat of the metronome ticking out the 302 seconds of totality." — Eddington, *Space, Time and Gravitation* (1920), via Lemos p. 36
- Eddington's notebook, 3 June 1919: "the one plate that I measure gave a result agreeing with Einstein." — Lemos, p. 36
- The joint Royal Society and Royal Astronomical Society meeting was on 6 November 1919, chaired by J. J. Thomson: "This is the most important result obtained in connection with the theory of gravitation since Newton's day." — *The Observatory* 42, 388 (1919), via Lemos p. 43
- *The Times*, 7 November 1919: "REVOLUTION IN SCIENCE / NEW THEORY OF THE UNIVERSE / NEWTONIAN IDEAS OVERTHROWN." — MPG (above)
- *New York Times*, 10 November 1919: "Lights All Askew in the Heavens… Einstein Theory Triumphs." — MPG (above)

## 12. The twin paradox and muons

- Einstein's 1905 paper already describes the key effect: a clock sent on a round trip comes back slow (§4, quoted in Beat 7). — Einstein 1905, fourmilab (above)
- Paul Langevin gave the traveller-and-stay-at-home story at the 1911 Bologna philosophy congress ("L'évolution de l'espace et du temps," *Scientia* 10, 1911). — arXiv:0811.3562 https://arxiv.org/abs/0811.3562
- **UNCERTAIN:** the exact numbers in Langevin's version (the traveller ages 2 years while 200 pass on Earth).
- Rossi & Hall, *Phys. Rev.* 59, 223 (1 Feb 1941), measured cosmic-ray muons at Echo Lake and Denver, Colorado, and found fast muons live longer, as time dilation predicts. — https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRev.59.223 ; APS Historic Sites: https://www.aps.org/programs/honors/history/historicsites/mtwashington.cfm
- Frisch & Smith, *Am. J. Phys.* 31, 342 (1963), counted muons on the summit of Mount Washington, New Hampshire, and near sea level in Cambridge, Massachusetts (about 1,900 m lower). — https://pubs.aip.org/aapt/ajp/article/31/5/342/1046834 ; APS Historic Sites
- Frisch & Smith counts: about 563 per hour at the top and about 408 at the bottom, where only about 27 would survive without time dilation. The dilation factor was about 8.8. **UNCERTAIN (exact counts vary by source)** — UNM summary: https://goldphysics.unm.edu/phys330/frisch-time-dilation.pdf
- Bailey et al., *Nature* 268, 301 (1977), CERN Muon Storage Ring: muons circling at γ = 29.33 lived about 64.4 µs instead of about 2.2 µs at rest, agreeing with Einstein to about 0.2%. — https://www.nature.com/articles/268301a0

## 13. Hafele–Keating (1971)

- In October 1971, Joseph Hafele (Washington University) and Richard Keating (U.S. Naval Observatory) flew four cesium-beam clocks around the world on ordinary commercial flights. — USNO/DVIDS: https://www.dvidshub.net/news/407785/usno-marks-50-year-anniversary-breakthrough-relativity-experiment
- Eastward trip 4–6 October 1971; westward trip 13–15 October 1971. — DVIDS (above)
- Eastward the clocks lost 59 ± 10 ns (predicted: lose 40 ± 23 ns). Westward they gained 273 ± 7 ns (predicted: gain 275 ± 21 ns). — *Science* 177, 166 & 168 (1972): https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.177.4044.168
- The Office of Naval Research grant was $8,000, mostly spent on airline tickets for the scientists and the clocks. — DVIDS (above)

## 14. Pound–Rebka (1959–60)

- R. V. Pound & G. A. Rebka Jr., "Apparent Weight of Photons," *Phys. Rev. Lett.* 4, 337, received 9 March 1960. — https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.4.337
- They used "the enclosed, isolated tower of the Jefferson Physical Laboratory" at Harvard, 74 ft (about 22.5 m) tall. — PRL 4, 337; APS Physics "The Weight of Light": https://physics.aps.org/story/v16/st1
- The light was the 14.4 keV gamma ray of iron-57 (the Mössbauer effect). The predicted frequency shift was about 2.5 × 10⁻¹⁵ each way. Result: 1.05 ± 0.10 times Einstein's prediction. — PRL 4, 337
- They sent the rays through a helium-filled Mylar bag so the air would not absorb them. — PRL 4, 337 (paper PDF: https://fisica.unipv.it/percorsi/pdf/rebka_1960.pdf)
- Accuracy improved to about 1% by 1964 (Pound & Snider). — APS Physics (above)

## 15. GPS

- Because of gravity, GPS satellite clocks run fast by about 45 µs per day. Because of their speed, they run slow by about 7 µs per day. Net: about 38 µs per day fast. — NIST: https://www.nist.gov/atomic-clocks/a-powerful-tool-for-science/putting-einstein-test ; R. Pogge, Ohio State: https://www.astronomy.ohio-state.edu/pogge.1/Ast162/Unit5/gps.html
- Ashby: uncorrected clock errors "building up in just one day would cause navigational errors of more than 11 km." — N. Ashby, "Relativity and the Global Positioning System," *Physics Today* 55(5), 41 (2002): https://physicstoday.aip.org/features/relativity-and-the-global-positioning-system
- Pogge's version: a position fix would be "false after only 2 minutes," with errors growing by about 10 km a day. — Ohio State (above)
- The fix is built in before launch: the satellite clocks are set about 4.465 parts in 10¹⁰ slow on the ground. — Ashby, *Physics Today* 2002
- When the first satellite with a cesium clock flew in 1977, engineers were unsure the correction was even needed. They built in a switch, ran the clock uncorrected for about three weeks, and measured an offset within 1% of Einstein's prediction. — Ashby, *Physics Today* 2002
- **UNCERTAIN:** the more precise 45.7 / 7.1 µs figures from Ashby's *Living Reviews in Relativity* (2003) paper; it could not be opened. Use 45 / 7 / 38.

## 16. Optical clocks: time dilation at human scale

- Chou, Hume, Rosenband & Wineland (NIST), "Optical Clocks and Relativity," *Science* 329, 1630 (24 September 2010): an aluminum-ion clock raised by 33 cm ran measurably faster. — https://www.science.org/doi/10.1126/science.1192720 ; NIST: https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2010/09/nist-pair-aluminum-atomic-clocks-reveal-einsteins-relativity-personal-scale
- They also measured time dilation at speeds of a few metres per second, about the pace of a car at 20 mph. — NIST 2010 (above)
- NIST's take: standing two steps higher on a staircase all your life would add about 90 billionths of a second over 79 years. — NIST: https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2010/09/nist-clock-experiment-demonstrates-your-head-older-your-feet
- Bothwell et al. (Jun Ye group, JILA), *Nature* 602, 420 (published 16 February 2022), measured the gravitational time difference across one cloud of about 100,000 strontium atoms, only a millimetre tall. The difference was about 1 part in 10¹⁹. — https://www.nature.com/articles/s41586-021-04349-7 ; NIST/JILA: https://www.nist.gov/news-events/news/2022/02/jila-atomic-clocks-measure-einsteins-general-relativity-millimeter-scale

## 17. Gravity Probe B and Schwarzschild

- Gravity Probe B launched 20 April 2004 and collected science data from August 2004 to August 2005. Results were announced 4 May 2011. — Everitt et al., *PRL* 106, 221101 (2011): https://link.aps.org/doi/10.1103/PhysRevLett.106.221101 ; Stanford: https://einstein.stanford.edu/highlights/status1.html
- Geodetic effect: 6,601.8 ± 18.3 mas/yr measured vs 6,606.1 predicted. Frame-dragging: 37.2 ± 7.2 mas/yr measured vs 39.2 predicted. — PRL 106, 221101; C. Will, APS *Physics* 4, 43: https://physics.aps.org/articles/v4/43
- The mission took about 47 years from idea to result and cost about $750 million. — APS *Physics* 4, 43
- The gyroscope rotors were 1.5-inch quartz spheres, listed as the roundest objects ever made. Enlarged to the size of the Earth, their tallest bump would be only about eight feet. — Stanford GP-B: http://einstein.stanford.edu/TECH/technology1.html
- Karl Schwarzschild volunteered in 1914 and served in Belgium, France and then on the Russian front, where he computed artillery trajectories. — MacTutor: https://mathshistory.st-andrews.ac.uk/Biographies/Schwarzschild/
- From the front he sent Einstein the first exact solution of the new equations in a letter dated 22 December 1915 (CPAE vol. 8, doc. 169). Einstein presented it to the Prussian Academy in January 1916. — arXiv:2312.01865 https://arxiv.org/html/2312.01865v1
- Einstein replied: "I had not expected that one could formulate the exact solution of the problem in such a simple way." — MacTutor (above)
- Schwarzschild's letter: the war "treated me kindly enough, in spite of the heavy gunfire, to allow me to get away from it all and take this walk in the land of your ideas." **UNCERTAIN (wording varies by translation)** — MPIWG virtual Einstein exhibition: http://einstein-virtuell.mpiwg-berlin.mpg.de/VEA/SC854034901_MOD1479463207_SEQ923184502_SL-692553921_en.html
- He caught the skin disease pemphigus at the front, was sent home in March 1916, and died in Potsdam on 11 May 1916, aged 42. — MacTutor

## 18. LIGO: GW150914

- Detected 14 September 2015 at 09:50:45 UTC. Livingston saw it about 7 milliseconds before Hanford. — Abbott et al., *PRL* 116, 061102 (2016): https://journals.aps.org/prl/abstract/10.1103/PhysRevLett.116.061102 ; LIGO Caltech: https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/news/ligo20160211
- Two black holes of about 36 and 29 solar masses merged into one of about 62. About 3 solar masses were radiated away as gravitational waves. — PRL 116, 061102
- Distance: about 410 Mpc, meaning the waves travelled about 1.3 billion years. — PRL; LIGO Caltech (above)
- At its peak the collision put out more power than all the stars in the visible universe combined; LIGO says about 50 times more. — LIGO Caltech (above)
- Each detector has two arms 4 km long. The peak strain was 1.0 × 10⁻²¹. — LIGO Caltech; PRL
- LIGO can sense a change in arm length smaller than one ten-thousandth of the width of a proton (10⁻¹⁹ m). This is a sensitivity figure, not the exact change caused by GW150914. — LIGO Facts: https://www.ligo.caltech.edu/page/facts
- Announced 11 February 2016. — LIGO Caltech (above)
- The signal arrived during an engineering run, four days before official observing was due to start on 18 September 2015. — LIGO/Virgo, arXiv:1602.03844 **UNCERTAIN (confirmed from abstract/summary only)**
- 2017 Nobel Prize in Physics: half to Rainer Weiss, half jointly to Barry Barish and Kip Thorne, "for decisive contributions to the LIGO detector and the observation of gravitational waves." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/2017/press-release/

## 19. Event Horizon Telescope

- The first image of a black hole, M87*, was released on 10 April 2019 in six papers in *ApJ Letters* 875. The black hole is 55 million light-years away and 6.5 billion solar masses. — ESO: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso1907/
- It used 8 radio telescopes worldwide observing at 1.3 mm in 2017, synchronised by hydrogen-maser atomic clocks. The data filled petabytes and were combined at MPIfR Bonn and MIT Haystack. — ESO eso1907
- One of the eight was the James Clerk Maxwell Telescope. — ESO eso1907
- Sagittarius A*, the Milky Way's own black hole, was imaged and released on 12 May 2022. It is about 4 million solar masses and 27,000 light-years away. Seen from Earth it is about the size of "a doughnut on the Moon." — ESO: https://www.eso.org/public/news/eso2208-eht-mw/

## 20. The Nobel that wasn't for relativity, and Einstein on wonder

- Einstein's Nobel is the 1921 Physics prize, "for his services to Theoretical Physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/einstein/facts/
- In 1921 the committee decided no nomination qualified, so the prize was reserved and awarded in November 1922. — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/summary/
- He missed the ceremony because he was sailing to Japan. At the banquet on 10 December 1922, German minister Rudolf Nadolny spoke for him. — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/einstein/speech/ ; Pal, arXiv:2112.13519
- His Nobel lecture, given in Gothenburg on 11 July 1923, was about relativity: "Fundamental ideas and problems of the theory of relativity." — https://www.nobelprize.org/prizes/physics/1921/einstein/lecture/
- Quote: "The most beautiful thing we can experience is the mysterious. It is the source of all true art and science. He to whom this emotion is a stranger, who can no longer pause to wonder and stand rapt in awe, is as good as dead: his eyes are closed." — Einstein, "What I Believe," *Forum and Century* 84 (October 1930); later translations say "fairest thing". Checked via https://wist.info/einstein-albert/5229/
- Quote: "It is almost a miracle that modern teaching methods have not yet entirely strangled the holy curiosity of inquiry." — AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early2.htm (Autobiographical Notes)
- Quote: "There was this huge world out there, independent of us human beings and standing before us like a great, eternal riddle… The contemplation of that world beckoned like a liberation." — AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early1.htm (Autobiographical Notes)
- Letter to Besso's family, 21 March 1955, after Besso died on 15 March. Einstein wrote that Besso "has departed from this strange world a little ahead of me," and that for believing physicists the distinction between past, present and future is only an illusion, "albeit a tenacious one." The popular "stubbornly persistent illusion" is Freeman Dyson's translation. Einstein died a month later, 18 April 1955. — Quote Investigator: https://quoteinvestigator.com/2024/03/18/stubborn-illusion/

## Small, surprising, human details

1. The Royal Society could not pay to print Newton's *Principia* because it had spent heavily on a lavish *History of Fishes*. Halley paid largely from his own pocket, and was later paid part of his salary in unsold copies of the fish book. — Royal Society: https://royalsociety.org/news/2012/The-fishy-blunder-that-nearly-prevented-Newtons-masterpiece-from-being-published/
2. As a boy at Edinburgh Academy, Maxwell was nicknamed "Dafty" for his country accent and home-made clothes. — Physics World: https://physicsworld.com/a/james-clerk-maxwell-a-force-for-physics/
3. Michelson's first ether experiment was ruined by Berlin street traffic, so he moved it to a windowless cellar in Potsdam. — "Michelson's first ether-drift experiment in Berlin and Potsdam" (Beat 4 link)
4. As a small boy Einstein built houses of cards many storeys high, and family legend says he was slow to start talking. — AIP: https://history.aip.org/exhibits/einstein/early1.htm
5. Einstein split his days into eight hours of patent work, eight hours of "allotria" (his own physics), and eight hours of sleep. — IGE: https://www.ige.ch/en/about-us/the-history-of-the-ipi/einstein/einstein-at-the-patent-office
6. He opened the letter announcing the miracle-year papers by calling his friend "you frozen whale." — Isaacson, *Einstein*, ch. 1 (excerpt link in Beat 7)
7. The most famous equation in physics is never written as "E=mc²" in the 1905 paper. It is "L/V²," in a note about three pages long. — original scan (Beat 7)
8. Minkowski, who united space and time, died less than four months after his famous lecture, aged 44. — Damour, arXiv:0807.1300
9. Before the eclipse trip, Dyson joked to Cottingham that if they found double the predicted bending, "Eddington will go mad, and you will have to come home alone." — Kennefick, arXiv:0709.0685 (quoting Chandrasekhar)
10. Eddington was a Quaker conscientious objector. In July 1918 a tribunal let him avoid war service on condition he carried on his work, which included the eclipse expedition. — Royal Observatory Greenwich: https://www.royalobservatorygreenwich.org/articles.php?article=1289
11. Asked what he would have done if the eclipse had gone against him, Einstein reportedly said he "would have to be sorry for the dear Lord. The theory is correct." **UNCERTAIN (a student's recollection years later, Ilse Rosenthal-Schneider)** — Lemos, arXiv:1912.05587, p. 41
12. Karl Schwarzschild solved Einstein's equations exactly while serving as an artillery officer on the Russian front, and died months later. — MacTutor (Beat 17)
13. Einstein and Leo Szilard received a U.S. patent on 11 November 1930 for a refrigerator with no moving parts, meant to stop deadly gas leaks in home fridges. — Smithsonian Lemelson Center: https://invention.si.edu/invention-stories/einstein-szilard-refrigerator
14. Einstein promised Mileva his Nobel Prize money as part of their divorce years before he won it. In 1923 he passed the whole prize to her, and she bought a house in Zurich. — Pal, arXiv:2112.13519 **UNCERTAIN (exact wording of the 1918 letter, CPAE vol. 8, doc. 449)**
15. The Hafele–Keating clocks needed their own airline seats; the grant was just $8,000. That the seats were booked under the name "Mr. Clock" is **UNCERTAIN (popular sources only; IOP Spark "The flight of 'Mr Clocks'")**. — DVIDS/USNO (Beat 13)
16. Pound and Rebka ran a helium-filled plastic bag up a Harvard lab tower so the air would not swallow their gamma rays. — PRL 4, 337
17. When the first GPS-era atomic clock went up in 1977, engineers were unsure the relativity correction was needed, so they built in a switch. Relativity was right within 1%. — Ashby, *Physics Today* 2002
18. "Your head is older than your feet": NIST measured time running faster just a foot higher. — NIST 2010 (Beat 16)
19. The Gravity Probe B gyroscopes are the roundest objects humans have made; only neutron stars are rounder. — Stanford GP-B (Beat 17)
20. The first gravitational wave arrived before the detectors were officially switched on for science, during a final engineering test. **UNCERTAIN (summary-level confirmation)** — arXiv:1602.03844
21. The Nobel lecture for a prize "not for relativity" was about relativity. — nobelprize.org (Beat 20)
