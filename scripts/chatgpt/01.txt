The first person to be surprised by a talking computer was a quiet professor at MIT, in Massachusetts, named Joseph Weizenbaum.

He had come a long way to get there. He was born in Berlin, in January nineteen twenty-three, the second son of a furrier. When he was twelve, his family had to leave Germany, because of the Nazi laws against Jewish people. They sailed from Bremen to America. He studied mathematics in Detroit, and during the Second World War he served in the American air forces as a weather forecaster. Later, at General Electric, he helped build one of the first computer systems for processing bank cheques.

So by the time he came to MIT, he knew computers very well. He knew exactly what they could do, and exactly what they couldn't.

In nineteen sixty-four and nineteen sixty-five, he wrote a little program. He called it ELIZA, after Eliza Doolittle, the flower seller in the play Pygmalion, who is taught to speak like a lady. He wrote that, like her, the program could be taught, and made to appear even more civilised.

ELIZA was very simple. It looked for key words in what you typed, and then it turned your own words around and handed them back to you, as a question. If you typed, I am sad, it might answer, how long have you been sad? And when it couldn't find any key word at all, it just said something gentle. Please go on. I see. Tell me more.

It was a bit like a certain kind of therapist. The kind who mostly listens, and asks you about your feelings.

There's a funny little detail in that paper, too. People typing to ELIZA couldn't use a question mark. On that old MIT computer, the question mark meant, delete this line. So every question had to be asked without one.

What surprised Weizenbaum wasn't the program. He knew exactly how simple it was. What surprised him was the people. His students, and others, became deeply absorbed in their conversations with it. Some told it very personal things. A colleague at MIT, Robert Fano, said later, it was amazing the extent that people did not understand they were talking to a computer.

He later told a story about his own secretary. She had watched him build the program for months. She knew perfectly well it was just a program. But when she first sat down to try it, after a few exchanges, she asked him to leave the room. So that she could keep talking to it in private. At least, that's how he remembered it.

Weizenbaum found it troubling. He wrote, in that same paper, that ELIZA shows how easy it is to create and maintain the illusion of understanding. A certain danger lurks there, he said.

Years later, he put it even more plainly. What I had not realised, he wrote, is that extremely short exposures to a relatively simple computer program could induce powerful delusional thinking in quite normal people.

Today this has a name. The ELIZA effect. Our very human habit of feeling that something understands us, the moment it speaks to us in words.

Weizenbaum spent the rest of his life thinking about it. He became one of the first and most persistent critics of handing too much of our lives to computers. In his later years, he was proud to call himself a heretic. He retired from MIT in nineteen eighty-eight, moved back to Germany in the nineties, and died in two thousand and eight, at the age of eighty-five, in a small town near Berlin.
