We tend to think that a machine like this reads words, the way we do. Or perhaps letters. But it doesn't read either. It reads tokens.

A token is a small piece of text. Sometimes it's a whole word. Sometimes it's part of a word. Sometimes just a single letter, or a comma, or a space.

OpenAI, the company that makes ChatGPT, has a simple rule of thumb for English. One token is about four letters. Or about three quarters of a word. So a hundred tokens is roughly seventy-five words. A short paragraph is about a hundred tokens.

So where do these pieces come from? Who decided how to cut the words up?

Nobody did, exactly. The pieces were found by a very old trick. And it began, of all places, as a way to make computer files smaller.

In February nineteen ninety-four, a programmer named Philip Gage published a short article in a computer magazine. It described a simple way to squeeze text into less space. Look for the pair of characters that appears most often, side by side. Replace that pair, everywhere, with a single new symbol. Then look again for the most common pair. And replace that. And again. And again.

It was called byte pair encoding. And for twenty years, it was just a small, clever compression trick.

Then, in twenty fifteen, three researchers at the University of Edinburgh, Rico Sennrich, Barry Haddow and Alexandra Birch, had a problem. They were building machines to translate languages. And their machines kept stumbling over rare words. Long words. Names. New words. Especially in German, where you can glue words together into enormous ones. Their example was the German word for a sewage water treatment plant. Abwasserbehandlungsanlage.

So they borrowed Gage's old trick. Start with single letters. Find the pair that appears most often in all your text. Glue it into one piece. Then find the next most common pair. Glue that. And keep going, thousands and thousands of times.

What you end up with is lovely, in its way. The most common words become single pieces. The. And. Night. Rarer words get built out of smaller, common parts. Like a child building a long word out of syllables. Abwasser. Behandlungs. Anlage.

Almost every chatbot today still cuts its text this way. GPT-2, in twenty nineteen, had a vocabulary of fifty thousand two hundred and fifty-seven pieces. Newer models have around a hundred thousand. Tokens have another, quieter side. They're not fair to every language.

The tokenisers were built mostly from English text. So English gets the big, efficient pieces. Other languages get cut into smaller bits. In Spanish, how are you, cómo estás, is five tokens for ten letters.
