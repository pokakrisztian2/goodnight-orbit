The little toy kite has drifted over to the window again. It has a small brass key tied to its string. It's bumping gently against the glass, as if it wants to go out and join the storm. Not tonight, little kite.

By seventeen fifty-two, Benjamin Franklin had a bold idea. If lightning really was electricity, then perhaps you could catch some of it. He suggested putting up a tall, pointed iron rod, standing on something that wouldn't let the electricity leak away, and waiting for a thundercloud to pass over it.

But he wasn't the first to try. That honour went to France.

In a village called Marly-la-Ville, about six leagues from Paris, a man named Dalibard followed Franklin's plan. He set up an iron rod, about an inch thick, forty feet long, and sharpened to a fine point. And he stood it on a small square board, held up by three wine bottles. I love that. One of the great experiments in history, balanced on three empty wine bottles.

Then Dalibard had to go away. So he left the rod in the care of an old soldier named Coiffier, who had served fourteen years in the dragoons. Someone you could trust to keep watch.

On Wednesday, the tenth of May, seventeen fifty-two, between two and three in the afternoon, Coiffier heard thunder. He hurried to the rod. And from it, he drew a small, bright spark.

Coiffier sent at once for the village priest, the Prior, a man named Raulet. And the Prior came running.

Now, picture the village. People look out of their windows, and they see their priest running down the road, as fast as he can, in the middle of a thunderstorm. And they think, oh no. Poor Coiffier has been killed by the thunder.

So they all ran after him. And then the hail came down. But, as the report puts it so sweetly, the hail did not stop the flock from following their shepherd.

When they arrived, out of breath and soaked, there was no tragedy at all. Just a soldier and a priest, drawing little sparks from the sky. The Prior drew several himself. Afterwards, he found a mark on his arm, like the lash of a wire.

Electricity and lightning were the same thing. A priest and an old soldier had shown it, with a little help from three wine bottles.

And what about Franklin? He was waiting, the story goes, for a church spire to be built in Philadelphia, so he could put his rod at the top. Then he thought of something easier. A kite.

He made it from two light strips of cedar, crossed, with a large, thin silk handkerchief stretched over them. Silk, he said, because it could bear the wet and wind of a thunderstorm without tearing. On top, a sharp pointed wire. At the bottom of the string, a key. And near his hand, a ribbon of silk, which he had to keep dry.

According to Joseph Priestley, who wrote the story down years later, with Franklin's help, Franklin told almost nobody what he was planning. He was afraid of being laughed at, if it didn't work. Only his son knew, and helped him raise the kite.

Now, in the old paintings, his son is usually a little boy. He wasn't. William Franklin was twenty-one. A grown man, probably feeling a bit awkward about the whole thing.

And there's another thing the paintings get wrong. Franklin did not stand out in the open, with lightning crashing down onto his kite. He stood under cover, in a doorway, or under some kind of roof, to keep the silk dry. And the kite was never struck by lightning.

What really happened was quieter. One promising cloud passed over, and nothing happened. Then, after a while, Franklin noticed the loose threads of the string beginning to stand up, and to lean away from each other. The same kind, pushing the same kind away. Just as Du Fay had said.

He brought his knuckle near the key. And he felt a spark.

The kite had simply been sitting quietly in the storm's electricity, gathering it, the way a wet cloth gathers rain. No bolt. No crash.

This happened in June seventeen fifty-two, about a month after Marly. But Franklin didn't know about France yet. He'd done it on his own.

There's one small doubt, and I'll tell you gently. When Franklin wrote about the kite in a Philadelphia newspaper that October, he wrote it like a set of instructions. Here's how anyone can try it. He never quite says, I did this. Most historians believe he did fly it. One writer has argued that he didn't. I like to think he did. Standing in a doorway, very still, waiting for a cloud.

Priestley called it perhaps the greatest discovery since the time of Isaac Newton. And in seventeen fifty-three, the Royal Society in London gave Franklin its Copley Medal, the same medal Stephen Gray had won, years before.

But Franklin was a practical man. So in his yearly almanac, he wrote out simple instructions. Take a small iron rod, he said. Push one end three or four feet into the damp ground. Let the other end stand six or eight feet above the highest part of the house. And on top, fix a foot of brass wire, about as thick as a knitting needle, sharpened to a fine point.

A house like that, he wrote, will not be damaged by lightning. The lightning is drawn to the point, and passes down through the metal, into the ground, without hurting anything.

That's still the idea today. Lightning tends to find tall, pointed, lonely things. So we give it a tall, pointed thing, made of metal, with a safe road down to the earth.

Not everyone who reached for the sky was so lucky. In Saint Petersburg, in seventeen fifty-three, a professor named Richmann had a rod that brought the electricity from the clouds right into his room. And one day, a flash came down it, and took his life. His companion, an engraver, said he saw a globe of blue fire, as big as his fist, jump from the rod. It was a quiet reminder, for everyone, that the sky was not a toy.

Down below my window, the storm is still glowing. From up here, it looks soft, like lanterns in a cloud.

My reading lamp is still on. Its light, too, is electricity. The same thing Coiffier caught in a spark, and Franklin caught on a key. But I still don't know why it comes on so quickly. We're not there yet.

For now, let's leave the sky. Let's go to Italy, to a quiet room, and to a table covered in frogs.
