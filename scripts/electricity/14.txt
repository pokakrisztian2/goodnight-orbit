The first part we already know. They really are slow. In an ordinary house wire, an electron might take more than half an hour to drift a single metre. And with alternating current, they hardly travel at all. They just rock gently back and forth, in place, fifty or sixty times a second.

But here's the second part. The wire isn't empty, waiting to be filled. It's already full of electrons, all the way along. Like a tube packed with marbles. Push one in at one end, and another drops out at the other, almost at once. Nobody has to make the whole journey.

And here's the third part, and the deepest. What actually races along the wire isn't the electrons at all. It's a change in the electric field. A ripple, spreading out at nearly the speed of light.

And the fourth part is the one that surprised even the great physicists. The energy that lights the lamp doesn't really travel inside the wire at all. It flows through the space around it. Richard Feynman, one of the great teachers of physics, wrote that the electrons get their energy from the field outside. Flowing inward, all around the wire, from the empty space beside it. It's clear, he wrote, that our ordinary intuitions are quite wrong.

And what is that field? It's the very thing Michael Faraday imagined, the bookbinder, with his invisible lines of force curving through space. And it's the same field James Clerk Maxwell wrote down in his equations, the field whose waves turned out to be light itself.

So when I touch my switch, the energy for my lamp is carried there by light's own cousin. Flowing round the outside of the wire, faster than any electron could ever crawl.
