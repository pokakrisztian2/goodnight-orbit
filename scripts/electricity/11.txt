On almost any street, you'll see it. A grey metal can, sitting quietly up on a wooden pole. It's called a transformer.

And the very first one was made by Michael Faraday, in eighteen thirty-one. You remember his iron ring, wound with wire made for bonnets. That ring was the first transformer ever made.

Here's how it works, as simply as I can say it.

Imagine two coils of wire, wound around the same piece of iron. They don't touch. When the current in the first coil changes, it makes a changing magnetic field in the iron. And that changing field, just as Faraday found, makes a voltage in the second coil.

If the second coil has more turns of wire than the first, you get more voltage. If it has fewer turns, you get less. Up, or down. That's all it does.

But notice one word. Changing. A transformer only works while the current keeps changing. Steady direct current doesn't change, so a transformer can't work on it. Alternating current changes all the time. It's made for transformers.

That's the whole secret of the War of the Currents.

But why would you want high voltage at all?

Because wires get warm. Some of the electricity is lost as heat along the way. And that heat grows with the square of the current. Double the current, and you get about four times the heat.

So here's the trick. If you raise the voltage, you can send the same amount of power with less current. Double the voltage, and you need only half the current. And half the current means only about a quarter of the heat.

So the power stations step the voltage up, very high. Today's long-distance lines carry hundreds of thousands of volts. The power travels across the country with very little lost. Then, near your home, the quiet box on the pole steps it back down again, gently, to something safe for a lamp.

And it works well. In the United States, only about five out of every hundred parts of the electricity are lost between the power station and the people using it.
