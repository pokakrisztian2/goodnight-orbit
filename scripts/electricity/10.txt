They're the kind of bulb people call Edison bulbs. And that brings us to a small correction, right at the start of this chapter.

Thomas Edison did not invent the first light bulb. The people who look after his old laboratory say so themselves, quite plainly. There were bulbs before his. The trouble was, they burned out after a few minutes. A lovely glow, and then darkness.

What Edison made was the first one that was practical. A lamp that would stay lit for hours and hours. And he built on the work of others, among them an English physicist named Joseph Swan.

So the question in his laboratory wasn't really how do you make light. It was how do you make light that lasts.

The answer was hidden inside the little wire. The filament. It had to glow without burning away. And so Edison's team, and a patient man named Charles Batchelor in particular, tried things. Platinum. Rubber. Even the black soot from kerosene lamps.

In the fall of eighteen seventy-nine, they tried a small cotton thread. First they carbonized it, burning it until it was hard. Then they sealed it in its glass bulb, and let it glow.

And it kept glowing. At least thirteen hours, by the careful count. Some books say even longer. Some books give a famous date for it, too, but the people who keep his old laboratory say new research has shown that date isn't right. I like that kind of honesty. History, still being tidied up.

Later, Batchelor found something better than cotton. Bamboo, from Japan.

I find that rather wonderful. A whole new age of light, held up for a while by a thin strip of carbonized bamboo.

But here is the part I think is most important. Edison didn't just make a bulb. Starting in the fall of eighteen seventy-eight, he spent thirty months building a whole system. Because a bulb on its own is no use at all. You need a place to make the electricity. Wires to carry it. And small, humble things too. Like the screw socket, to hold the lamp in its fitting. And fuses, to stop the wires from overloading, and to keep fires from starting.

Now, the very first central station for lighting with these lamps wasn't in America at all. It was in London. At Holborn Viaduct, it started up on the twelfth of January, eighteen eighty-two. But it was only meant to be temporary.

The one that's remembered as the real beginning came later that year, in New York.

At two hundred and fifty-seven Pearl Street, there was a building full of big machines. Dynamos. They were huge. Each one weighed about twenty-seven tons, and they were nicknamed Jumbo, after a famous circus elephant of the day. Each Jumbo could light about twelve hundred lamps.

On the fourth of September, eighteen eighty-two, Edison wasn't at the power station. He was standing in the office of one of his investors, the banker J. P. Morgan. And from there, he gave the signal to his chief electrician, John Lieb, to close the switch.

And in a small corner of the city, about a quarter of a square mile, the lamps came on.

But people noticed soon enough. Within a year, the station was lighting about ten thousand lamps, for five hundred and thirteen customers.

The electricity at Pearl Street was what we call direct current. It flowed one way, steadily, at about a hundred and ten volts. The wires ran underground, wrapped and sealed with beeswax, and linseed oil, and asphalt. A little like a carefully waxed candle, buried beneath the street.

And it was remarkably faithful. Pearl Street ran from that September day until January eighteen ninety, more than seven years, with only one interruption. It lasted three hours.

In eighteen ninety there was a fire. But one of the old dynamos survived it. They call it old number nine, and today it sits quietly in the Henry Ford Museum.

And the place where the station once stood, at Pearl Street? It's a parking lot now. There's something gentle about that, I think. Cars resting in the dark where the first lights began.

Even so, electric light came slowly to most people's homes. It wasn't until nineteen twenty-five that half of all homes in the United States had electric power. More than forty years after Pearl Street.
