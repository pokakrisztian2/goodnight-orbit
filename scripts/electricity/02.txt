On the shelf behind me, there's a row of glass jars. Tall ones, lined with metal foil, inside and out, with a little brass rod poking up through the lid, and a small ball on top. They look like something you'd keep jam in. But they have a history. And a bit of a temper.

Joseph Priestley, looking back years later, said that the end of seventeen forty-five, and the beginning of seventeen forty-six, were famous for the most surprising discovery that had yet been made in the whole business of electricity.

It began with a quiet churchman in Pomerania. His name was Ewald von Kleist, and he was the dean of the cathedral in a town called Camin. On the fourth of November, seventeen forty-five, he wrote a letter to a doctor in Berlin, describing something strange.

He had taken a narrow-necked medicine bottle, and put a nail into it. Then he charged the nail with electricity. And when he touched it, he got a shock. A real one. Far stronger than anyone expected from a little bottle.

But there was a catch. In a second letter, that December, he wrote that what really surprised him was that the powerful effect only happened when he was holding the bottle in his hand.

And that was the part he hadn't explained clearly. So people tried it, and nothing much happened. Nobody could repeat it. The poor dean had found something wonderful, and left out the one small detail that made it work. I think we've all written instructions like that.

A few months later, early in seventeen forty-six, the same thing happened again, by chance, in the Dutch city of Leiden. This time the person holding the bottle was a man named Musschenbroek. And this time, the news spread.

He wrote about it in a letter, in French. As Priestley later retold it, he felt himself struck in his arms, and his shoulder, and his chest, so that he lost his breath. And it took him two days to get over the blow, and the fright. He said, more or less, that he would not take a second shock for the whole kingdom of France.

I find that a very honest thing to say. Not, this is a great discovery. Just, please, never again.

Because of Leiden, the bottle came to be called the Leyden jar. Priestley said it was named after a man named Cuneus, a native of Leiden, who had made it.

So what was the jar doing? It's a bit like filling a bucket with water from a slow tap, a little at a time, and then tipping it out all at once. The jar let people gather electricity slowly, and keep it for a while, and then let it go in one sudden snap.

And once people knew how to make one, it became, as Priestley put it, the subject of general conversation. All over Europe, people earned a living by travelling from town to town, showing it off.

In France, there was a showman of science called the Abbé Nollet. In seventeen forty-six, in front of the king at Versailles, he linked a hundred and eighty royal guards together, in a line. Then he let the jar go through them. And all hundred and eighty jumped at once. I like to imagine their faces afterwards, trying to look dignified.

In another French trial, a long chain of men, each holding a piece of iron wire to the next, stretched nine hundred toises. A toise was an old French measure. That chain was roughly a mile long. And every one of them felt it.

There's one more story, told by Leiden University today. They say the shows reached their peak with seven hundred monks, joined together in a circle. I like to picture them. Seven hundred monks, very quiet, and then all of them hopping at the same moment.

Meanwhile, across the ocean, in Philadelphia, a man named Benjamin Franklin had become fascinated. His interest had started in seventeen forty-three, after he watched a show by a travelling lecturer.

Remember Du Fay, with his two kinds of electricity? Franklin pictured it differently. He imagined just one kind of electric fire. Some things had too much of it. Some things had too little.

And in a letter to a friend, Peter Collinson, on the twenty-fifth of May, seventeen forty-seven, he wrote down some new words. We say, he wrote, that this body is electrised positively, and that one negatively. Or rather, plus and minus.

And then, a lovely little line. We daily, in our experiments, electrise bodies plus or minus, as we think proper.

Plus and minus. That's where they come from. Those two small marks on every battery you've ever held go all the way back to a man in Philadelphia, choosing names for something nobody could see. He had to guess which side had more, and which had less. It was a guess. We'll find out, later tonight, how that guess turned out.

And the jar puzzled him completely. That summer, he wrote about how the plus and the minus were combined and balanced in this miraculous bottle, in a way, he said, that I can by no means comprehend.

One of the cleverest men of his century, writing, I don't understand this. I find that very comforting.

And then there's the turkey.

On Christmas Day, seventeen fifty, Franklin wrote a letter that begins, I have lately made an experiment in electricity that I desire never to repeat.

He had been planning to use the shock from two large glass jars to kill a turkey. Between them, those jars held as much electric fire as forty ordinary ones. And somehow, instead of the turkey, he took the whole shock himself.

He wrote that the flash was very great, and the crack as loud as a pistol. Yet, he said, his senses were gone so quickly that he neither saw the one, nor heard the other.

He recovered. But he was very embarrassed. And at the end of the letter, he asked, please, do not make it more public. For I am ashamed to have been guilty of so notorious a blunder.

Well. I'm sorry, Benjamin. Here we are, telling everyone.

Outside my window, the storm is still glowing softly, deep inside the clouds. Franklin had started to wonder about storms like that. He had begun to suspect that the little sparks in his jars, and the great lightning in the sky, might be the very same thing.

And he wasn't the only one who wanted to find out.
