Albert Einstein was born in Ulm, in Germany, on the fourteenth of March, eighteen seventy-nine. He grew up in Munich, in a family that ran a small electrical business.

He was a quiet child. Family stories say he was slow to start talking. And he could be very patient with things that interested him. He would build houses of cards, many stories high, carefully, one card at a time, while other children would have given up.

When he was about four or five, he was sick in bed, and his father brought him a compass to play with.

Maybe you had one as a child. A little round case, a needle floating inside. And whichever way you turn it, the needle swings back and points the same direction. As if an invisible hand is holding it.

The little boy was amazed. Nothing was touching the needle. And yet something was pushing it. Something in the empty air. Many years later, as an old man, he wrote that the compass made him feel that there had to be something behind things, something deeply hidden.

I like to think of that little boy, lying in bed, turning the compass around and around, trying to catch the needle out. And the needle, patiently, pointing north every time.

At about twelve, he found a small geometry book. He later called it a holy little geometry booklet. It was full of proofs, arguments that start from simple things and arrive, step by step, at something certain. He said this lucidity and certainty made an indescribable impression on him.

So he had two wonders, early on. The mystery of the invisible force in the compass. And the clarity of mathematics that could explain things with complete certainty. His whole life, in a way, would be about bringing those two wonders together.

But school was a different matter.

In Munich he went to the Luitpold Gymnasium, a strict German school where students memorized, recited, and obeyed. He hated it. He didn't like being drilled. He didn't like authority. And his teachers, in turn, didn't much like him. One teacher reportedly told him his very presence in the classroom was disrespectful.

When he was fifteen, his family's business moved to Italy. They left him behind in Munich to finish school. He lasted until December of eighteen ninety-four. Then, in the middle of the school year, he simply left, and went to join his parents in Italy.

A teenager, without a diploma, crossing the Alps to his family.

The next year, he tried to enter the Polytechnic in Zurich, a famous technical university in Switzerland, even though he was younger than most students. And he failed the entrance exam. He did very well in mathematics and physics. But he did poorly in languages and history.

So he was sent to a small school in a Swiss town called Aarau, to finish his studies. And Aarau turned out to be exactly what he needed.

It was a gentle school. The teachers encouraged students to think for themselves, to look, to wonder, to ask. No drilling. No shouting. He was happy there. He stayed with a kind family. He took long walks. He played his violin.

And it was there, in Aarau, at about sixteen years old, that he first had the question we started with tonight.

Many years later, he described it himself. He said he had hit upon a paradox at the age of sixteen. If I pursue a beam of light with the velocity c, the velocity of light in a vacuum, I should observe such a beam of light as an electromagnetic field at rest, though spatially oscillating. There seems to be no such thing, however.

Let's unpack that slowly, because it's the heart of everything.

Light is a wave. A ripple of electricity and magnetism, moving forward. Now imagine you're running alongside it. Faster and faster. Until you're moving at exactly the speed of light, right next to the wave.

What would you see?

If you ride next to a train at the same speed, the train looks like it's standing still. You could reach out and touch the window. So if you ride next to a light wave at the same speed, the wave should look like it's standing still too. Frozen. Crests and troughs, just hanging there in space, not moving. Like an ocean swell caught in a photograph.

But young Einstein had a feeling this was wrong. Nobody had ever seen frozen light. And Maxwell's equations didn't allow it. In Maxwell's equations, light always moves. It never stands still. There is no such thing as a resting wave of light.

So either the equations were wrong, or the simple idea of catching up with light was wrong.

He didn't solve it then. He just carried it. For years. Like a small stone in his pocket that he'd turn over, now and then, when his mind wandered.

At Aarau he also wrote a school essay, in French, about his plans for the future. He wrote that he wanted to study physics. And one of the reasons he gave was, and I promise this is true, his lack of imagination and practical talent.

The man who would become famous for his imagination, at sixteen, said he didn't have much.

In eighteen ninety-six, he passed his exams and entered the Zurich Polytechnic after all. He studied there for four years, to become a teacher of mathematics and physics.

He was not a model student. He skipped many lectures. He preferred to read the great physicists on his own, at home, and to do experiments in the laboratory. He found some of his professors old-fashioned.

But he had a friend. A classmate named Marcel Grossmann. Grossmann went to every lecture and took beautiful, careful notes. And before exams, he lent those notes to Einstein. Einstein later wrote, I would rather not speculate on what would have become of me without these notes.

Remember Grossmann. He comes back in our story later, at a very important moment.

There was also a young woman in his physics section. The only woman in the group. Her name was Mileva Marić. She had come from Serbia, from a small town called Titel, and she was determined to study physics at a time when very few women were allowed to.

She and Albert became close. They studied together. They argued about physics together. They wrote long letters when they were apart. In one letter, in March of nineteen oh one, he wrote to her, how happy and proud I will be when the two of us together will have brought our work on relative motion to a victorious conclusion.

How much Mileva contributed to his later ideas is something historians still discuss, with care, and without full agreement. Some say there's no clear evidence she added to the physics. Others say their early thinking was so intertwined that it's hard to separate. What we do know is that she was there, in those years, thinking about the same things. They married in Bern, in January of nineteen oh three.

After graduating in nineteen hundred, Einstein looked for a job at a university. Any university. He wrote letters to professors all over Europe. Nobody hired him. He tutored students. He did short teaching jobs. He was poor, and unsure, and a little lost.

Then, once again, his friend Grossmann helped. Grossmann's father knew the director of the Swiss patent office in Bern. A recommendation was made.

And in June of nineteen oh two, the young man who had been chasing a beam of light in his mind since he was sixteen, sat down at a desk, in an office, to read other people's inventions.
