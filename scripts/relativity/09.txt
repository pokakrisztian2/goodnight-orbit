Normally, you can't see stars near the Sun. The Sun is far too bright. The sky around it is washed out, pale blue, and every star behind it is hidden.

But during a total eclipse, for a few minutes, the Moon slides perfectly in front of the Sun. The sky goes dark. The air cools. Birds go quiet, thinking it's evening. And in that strange, silver twilight, the stars come out, even the ones sitting right beside the hidden Sun.

If Einstein was right, those stars near the edge of the Sun would appear slightly out of place. Their light, bending as it passed through the Sun's curved spacetime, would make them look a little farther from the Sun than they really were. Compare a photograph taken during the eclipse, with a photograph of the same stars taken at night, months later, when the Sun is somewhere else in the sky. And measure the shift.

Einstein's prediction was one point seven five arcseconds at the very edge of the Sun. Twice as much as a simpler, older calculation based on Newton's ideas would give. So the eclipse could do more than show that light bends. It could tell which theory was right.

There was a total eclipse coming on the twenty-ninth of May, nineteen nineteen. And it was a very good one. The Sun would be sitting in front of a bright cluster of stars, the Hyades, which meant plenty of stars to measure.

In Britain, the Astronomer Royal, Frank Dyson, organized two expeditions. One would go to Sobral, a town in northern Brazil. The other would go to Príncipe, a small island off the west coast of Africa. Two places, in case one had clouds.

Leading the Príncipe team was an astronomer named Arthur Eddington. And Eddington is a lovely part of this story.

He was a Quaker, deeply committed to peace. During the First World War, which had only just ended, he had refused to fight. In the summer of nineteen eighteen, a tribunal allowed him to avoid war service, on the condition that he kept doing his scientific work. And part of that work, it turned out, was this eclipse.

Before the teams left, there's a story that Dyson teased the other astronomer on Eddington's team, a clockmaker named Cottingham. If they found double the predicted bending, Dyson joked, Eddington will go mad, and you will have to come home alone.

On Príncipe, the morning of the eclipse brought bad weather. Rain, and then heavy clouds. Eddington and Cottingham waited, watching the sky, hoping. As the eclipse began, the clouds were still there.

Eddington later described those minutes. In his words, they took sixteen photographs, with, as he wrote, the beat of the metronome ticking out the three hundred and two seconds of totality.

Three hundred and two seconds. A little over five minutes. A metronome ticking, so they could time their photographs without looking up. The sky dark, the clouds drifting, and two men working as fast and as carefully as they could, not even able to see whether the stars were there.

When they developed the plates, most of them showed only clouds. But a few showed stars.

A few days later, on the third of June, Eddington measured one of those plates. And he wrote in his notebook, the one plate that I measure gave a result agreeing with Einstein.

Over in Brazil, the Sobral team had clearer skies, and took more photographs. One of their instruments had trouble with focus, perhaps from the heat of the Sun on the equipment. But the other, a smaller telescope with a four-inch lens, gave beautiful, sharp images.

Back in England, over the summer and autumn, the plates were measured carefully. The results from the smaller telescope at Sobral, and from Príncipe, were both close to Einstein's prediction. Much closer to his value than to the old, smaller one.

On the sixth of November, nineteen nineteen, a special joint meeting of the Royal Society and the Royal Astronomical Society was held in London. The room was full. On the wall hung a portrait of Isaac Newton.

The results were presented. And the chairman, the physicist J. J. Thomson, the man who had discovered the electron, said, this is the most important result obtained in connection with the theory of gravitation since Newton's day.

The next day, the London newspaper, The Times, ran the headline, Revolution in Science. New Theory of the Universe. Newtonian Ideas Overthrown.

And almost overnight, Albert Einstein, the former patent clerk, became the most famous scientist in the world. Newspapers everywhere wrote about the strange new theory where space bends and time stretches. Most people didn't understand it at all. But they loved the idea of it. After a long, dark war, here was something wondrous. A reminder that the universe was bigger and stranger than anyone had imagined.

Now here's a small twist. You might think the eclipse, and all that fame, would have brought Einstein a Nobel Prize for relativity. It didn't.

Relativity was still controversial with some members of the prize committee. So when Einstein finally received the Nobel Prize in Physics, the one for nineteen twenty-one, which was actually awarded a year late, in November of nineteen twenty-two, it was for his services to theoretical physics, and especially for his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect. The paper from March of nineteen oh five. The one about light coming in little packets.

He wasn't even at the ceremony. He was on a ship, sailing to Japan. At the banquet in Stockholm, the German envoy, Rudolf Nadolny, spoke on his behalf.

And then, the next summer, in July of nineteen twenty-three, in Gothenburg, in Sweden, Einstein finally gave his Nobel lecture. And what did he choose to speak about?

Relativity.

The prize they wouldn't give him for it, and he spoke about it anyway. Quietly, politely, and completely.
