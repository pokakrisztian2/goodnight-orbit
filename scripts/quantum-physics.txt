Complete units. Whole packets.

Now the puzzle solves itself, like a key turning in a lock.

A blue packet is bigger. It pays the fee easily, and there's plenty left over. The electron flies out fast.

But it can jump. From a higher step down to a lower one. And when it does, it gives off one packet of light. One photon. And the energy of that photon is exactly the height of the drop.

Bohr admitted, in his paper, that this was in obvious contrast to the ordinary ideas of physics. But it seemed necessary, he wrote, in order to account for experimental facts.

It isn't really about clumsy measurements, or poking things too hard. The blurriness is built into the particle itself. An electron simply doesn't have an exact position and an exact motion at the same time. Not because we can't see them. Because they aren't there to be seen.

Here's a gentle way to picture it. Think of a single musical note. If you want to know its pitch very precisely, you need to hear it for a while. A long, steady hum. But then it isn't happening at any one exact moment. It's spread out in time. And if you make the note very short, a quick little click, it happens at one sharp moment, but then its pitch becomes fuzzy and hard to name. Sharp in time, or sharp in pitch. Never both at once.

Particles are like that, in their own way. Because, as de Broglie saw, they're waves too.

Heisenberg himself never liked the word principle. He called them inaccuracy relations, or indeterminacy relations. The name uncertainty principle was given by others, in English, a couple of years later. And it stuck.

He received the Nobel Prize for creating quantum mechanics. He later wrote to Born and Jordan that he was sorry the three of them had not shared it.

So now we have one version of quantum mechanics. Tables of numbers, with no pictures at all. Very powerful. Very strange. And very cold.

But at almost exactly the same time, far away in the Swiss mountains, someone else was finding a completely different way to say the same thing. And his version was full of waves.

Erwin Schrödinger was an Austrian physicist, working in Zürich, in Switzerland. And in late nineteen twenty-five, after he'd given a talk about de Broglie's electron waves, a colleague, Peter Debye, made a remark that stayed with him. If electrons are waves, Debye said, then you ought to have a wave equation. A proper mathematical rule for how the waves behave.

At Christmas that year, Schrödinger went on holiday to Arosa, a mountain resort in the Swiss Alps. Snow. Pine trees. Clean, cold air. And he later told a colleague that he'd hardly gone skiing at all, because he was busy with a few calculations.

A letter he wrote from Arosa, dated the twenty-seventh of December, shows what those calculations were. He was treating the hydrogen atom as a wave.

The story goes, as a colleague remembered it many years later, that when Schrödinger came back to Zürich, he began his next talk with the words, my colleague Debye suggested that one should have a wave equation. Well, I have found one.

In nineteen twenty-six, he published it, in a series of four papers. And it's one of the most famous equations in all of science. The Schrödinger equation.

Here's the lovely thing about it. Physicists could picture it. After Heisenberg's cold tables of numbers, this felt like coming in from the snow to a warm room. Waves were familiar. Everyone had studied them. Sound waves. Water waves. Vibrating strings.

And here's the image that made it all make sense. Think of a guitar string, fixed at both ends. When you pluck it, it can't vibrate any way it likes. It can only vibrate in certain patterns. And there's nothing in between. No pattern with one and a half sections.

That's the staircase again. Planck's steps. Bohr's steps. Now they had a reason. An electron wave, held inside an atom, is like a string held at both ends. It can only settle into certain patterns. And each pattern has its own energy. That's why the steps exist.

Schrödinger's equation calculated the energy levels of the hydrogen atom exactly. Matching the colours of Balmer's lines, from that school in Basel, perfectly.

Now, Schrödinger didn't much like Heisenberg's version of the theory. He said the tables of numbers left him discouraged, not to say repelled. And Heisenberg, in a private letter to Pauli, was rather less polite about Schrödinger's waves. The more I think about it, he wrote, the more repulsive I find it.

So you might think they'd found two different theories, and one of them must be wrong.

But in nineteen twenty-six, Schrödinger showed something surprising. Both versions give exactly the same answers. Every time. They're the same theory, written in two different languages. Like the same poem, in German and in French. One in tables. One in waves.

But that left a question hanging in the air, like mist over a lake. What exactly is waving?

A water wave is water moving. A sound wave is air moving. But an electron wave? What is it made of? Schrödinger hoped at first that the electron itself was somehow smeared out, spread thin like butter across space. But that didn't work. Remember the double slit. Every electron arrives as one single dot. Never smeared.

The answer came from Max Born. The same Born who'd recognised Heisenberg's matrices.

The wave, Born said, is not the electron. The wave tells you where the electron is likely to be found.

Where the wave is big, you're likely to find the electron. Where the wave is small, you're unlikely to. Where the wave is zero, you'll never find it there at all. The wave is a wave of chance. A map of possibilities.

Born wrote it in a small note, added to his paper at the last minute, in the summer of nineteen twenty-six. It's one of the most important footnotes ever written.

Go back to the double slit, with this in mind. The electron's wave passes through both slits. On the other side, the two parts of the wave overlap, and make stripes. Big in some places, small in others. That's a map of where the electron is likely to land. Then, when it arrives at the screen, the electron appears as one dot, in one place, chosen by chance, according to the map. Each dot is a surprise. But thousands of dots together draw the map exactly.

It's a little like rain falling on a roof at night. You can't say where the next drop will land. But after a long rain, you can see exactly where the gutters overflow, and where the ground stays dry.

This was a profound change. Before, physics had always been a matter of certainty. If you knew exactly how things were now, you could, in principle, predict exactly what would happen next. Like clockwork.

Now, at the very bottom of nature, physics could only tell you the chances. Not what will happen. Only what might.

Born took the idea partly from Einstein, who'd once had a similar thought about light. But, as we're about to see, Einstein did not like where this was going. Not at all.

Born received his Nobel Prize for this idea in nineteen fifty-four, almost thirty years later. Schrödinger received his in nineteen thirty-three, sharing it with a very quiet English physicist named Paul Dirac.

Dirac was famous for saying very little. It's said that his colleagues in Cambridge joked about a unit of speech in his honour, a Dirac, which was about one word per hour. But in nineteen twenty-eight, he wrote an equation that combined quantum waves with Einstein's relativity. And it predicted something nobody had ever seen. A twin of the electron, exactly the same, but with the opposite charge.

A few years later, it was found. The positron. The first hint of antimatter. Discovered, in a way, by a man who hardly spoke, simply by listening very carefully to his mathematics.

Einstein had helped to start quantum physics. His packets of light were one of its first great ideas. But as the new theory grew, he became more and more uneasy about it.

It was the chance that troubled him. The idea that, at the very bottom of nature, things happen for no reason at all. That an electron lands here, rather than there, simply by luck.

In December of nineteen twenty-six, he wrote a letter to his friend Max Born. And it contains a sentence that became famous, though it's usually quoted a little differently. The theory yields a lot, he wrote, but it hardly brings us any closer to the secret of the Old One. In any case, I am convinced that he does not throw dice.

God does not play dice. That's the way people usually remember it.

Einstein disagreed. And here's the part of the story I find most charming. The famous argument between Einstein and Bohr didn't mostly happen in the lecture hall. In the official record of the meetings, both of them are actually rather quiet. It happened over breakfast.

Heisenberg remembered it later. We generally met already at breakfast in the hotel, he said, and Einstein began to describe an imaginary experiment. Day after day. Breakfast, puzzle. Dinner, answer.

Three years later, at the next Solvay conference, in nineteen thirty, Einstein came with his cleverest puzzle of all.

Imagine a box, he said, full of light. On one side of the box, a tiny shutter, controlled by a clock inside. At an exact moment, the shutter opens, and lets out a single photon. Then closes again. So you know exactly when the photon left.

Now weigh the box, before and after. The box is a tiny bit lighter afterwards. And from Einstein's own famous equation, E equals m c squared, the lost weight tells you exactly how much energy the photon carried away.

So you know both the exact time, and the exact energy. And the uncertainty principle says you can't have both.

It's said that Bohr was shaken, and spent the evening going from one person to another, sure it couldn't be right, but unable to say why.

But by the next day, he had the answer. And it's one of the most beautiful answers in the history of physics. Because Bohr used Einstein's own theory against him. To weigh the box, it has to hang on a spring, in gravity. When the photon leaves, the box moves slightly. And as we learned in the relativity night, clocks run at different speeds at different heights in gravity. So the moment the box moves, its clock changes its rate, just a little. Just enough to blur the time. Exactly enough to keep the uncertainty principle safe.

Einstein's relativity had rescued the quantum rule that Einstein was trying to break.

Bohr later wrote that, at the end of the discussion, to which Einstein himself contributed effectively, it became clear that the argument could not be upheld.

But I want you to notice something about all this. It was never bitter. Bohr said a most humorous spirit animated the discussions. When the two men had first met, years before, Einstein reportedly wrote to Bohr that not often in life has a human being caused me such joy by his mere presence as you did. They disagreed about the deepest questions in the universe, for the rest of their lives. And they stayed friends.

Einstein never fully accepted the new theory. He believed there must be a real world out there, independent of anyone looking at it. He thought quantum mechanics was right, as far as it went, but incomplete. That something was missing.

Bohr's view came to be called the Copenhagen interpretation, after the city where he worked. Very roughly, it says we shouldn't ask what an electron is doing when nobody's measuring it. We should just use the rules, which work perfectly, and describe what we actually observe.

Not everyone was satisfied with that, then or now. Other ideas have come along over the years. One says that every possible outcome really happens, each one in its own branching world. Another, which goes back to de Broglie himself, says particles always have real positions, and are guided along by their waves, like little boats on a current.

Physicists still disagree about which picture is right, if any. And for ordinary experiments, the main pictures all predict the same results. So nobody has yet found a way to settle it.

The argument that began over breakfast in Brussels, a hundred years ago, is still going on. Quietly. Politely. At breakfast tables all over the world.

In the summer of nineteen thirty-five, Einstein and Schrödinger were writing letters to each other. Both of them were uneasy about where quantum physics had gone. And they were trying to find the clearest way to show what bothered them.

Einstein suggested an example. Imagine a pile of gunpowder, he wrote, which might explode at some point within the next year, or might not, depending on some tiny quantum event. According to the new theory, until someone checks, its description would have to mix the exploded and the unexploded together. Which, he thought, was plainly absurd.

Schrödinger took the idea, and made it into something much more memorable. He put it in a long article, published later that year. And in the middle of it, there's a paragraph that has become the most famous thought experiment in all of physics.

I'll read you a little of it. Don't worry. It's only imaginary. No animals were ever involved.

One can even set up quite ridiculous cases, Schrödinger wrote. A cat is penned up in a steel chamber, along with the following diabolical device, which must be secured against direct interference by the cat.

I've always loved that little detail. Secured against direct interference by the cat. He knew cats.

Inside the chamber, there's a tiny amount of radioactive material. So little that, in the course of an hour, perhaps one of its atoms breaks apart, or perhaps none does. Equal chances. If an atom does break apart, a detector notices it, and through a little mechanism, releases a hammer, which breaks a small bottle of poison.

After an hour, has the atom broken apart or not? Quantum physics says that, until something checks, the atom is in a kind of combination of both. A superposition. Broken and not broken, both at once, with equal weight.

And then, Schrödinger pointed out, if you follow the theory strictly, the cat must be in a combination too. The whole system, he wrote, would have in it the living and the dead cat, and then he added, in brackets, pardon the expression, mixed or smeared out in equal parts.

Pardon the expression. He was a very polite man.

Now, here's the thing people often forget. Schrödinger didn't mean this as a description of how the world works. He meant it as a joke. A criticism. He was saying, look, it's fine to talk about blurry, smeared-out states for atoms. But if your theory says the same about a cat, something's gone badly wrong. Blurring, he wrote, seems simply wrong for things we can see and touch.

He was trying to make the idea look ridiculous. Instead, he made it famous. People have been drawing that cat, in its box, ever since.

So why don't we ever see a cat that's both alive and dead? Or a teacup that's in two places at once? Or anything big, in a superposition?

The best answer we have today has a rather technical name. Decoherence. But the idea is gentle, and I think you'll like it.

A single electron, travelling through an empty vacuum, is very lonely. Nothing touches it. Nothing notices it. And so it can stay in its strange combination of possibilities, passing through both slits, keeping its options open.

But a cat is never lonely like that. A cat is constantly being touched by the world. It breathes air. It's warm, so it gives off heat. Light bounces off its fur. Dust settles on it. Countless tiny particles hit it every moment, and each one carries away a little bit of information about where the cat is, and what it's doing.

In a sense, the whole world is always watching the cat. Not with eyes. Just by touching it. And, like the electron in the double slit when we peeked, all that quiet watching washes away the strange quantum combinations, almost instantly.

How quickly? Scientists have worked it out. For a single speck of dust, floating in ordinary air, the quantum blurriness, even on scales far smaller than an atom, is washed away in about a billionth of a second. For a cat, far, far faster. So quickly that there's no hope of ever catching it in two states at once.

That's why the everyday world looks so solid and certain. Not because the quantum rules stop working for big things. They don't. But because big things are never alone. The world is always gently touching them, and in touching, deciding.

There's a lovely thought in that, I think. The strange quantum world is still there, underneath everything. But it's the constant, quiet contact between things that makes the world feel steady and real.

In twenty twelve, two physicists, Serge Haroche and David Wineland, won the Nobel Prize for learning how to hold single particles, and single atoms, perfectly still and isolated, without destroying their quantum states. The Nobel committee wrote that single particles lose their mysterious quantum properties as soon as they interact with the outside world. What those two scientists did was build little boxes of silence, where the quantum world could survive long enough to be studied.

Now, I should be honest with you. Decoherence explains why we never see superpositions of big things. But the philosophers who study this point out, quite rightly, that it doesn't fully explain why we see one particular result, rather than another. Why the dot lands here, and not there. That part of the mystery is still open.

In the spring of nineteen thirty-five, the same year as the cat, Einstein published one more puzzle. He wrote it with two young colleagues at Princeton, Boris Podolsky and Nathan Rosen. So it's known by their three initials. E, P, R.

Its title was a question. Can the quantum description of physical reality be considered complete? And their answer, at the end, was no.

Here's the idea, in the gentlest way I can put it.

Imagine a little machine that throws two balls at once, in opposite directions. One is always black, and the other is always white. But you don't know which is which. You stand at one end, and a friend stands far away at the other. You catch your ball. It's black. And straight away, without asking, you know your friend is holding a white one.

Nothing strange about that. The balls were black and white all along. You just didn't know which was which, until you looked.

Now, quantum physics says that for tiny particles, it's different. It says that, before anyone looks, neither ball has a colour yet. It's as if both are grey. Right up until the moment someone looks at one of them. And then, at that instant, one turns black, and the other, however far away, turns white.

Einstein couldn't accept that. How could looking at one particle here decide something about another particle, far away, instantly, without anything travelling between them? Surely, he thought, each particle must have carried its answer with it, all along. Like a little sealed envelope with instructions inside. And if the theory couldn't see those envelopes, the theory was incomplete.

Those secret instructions came to be called hidden variables.

Einstein wasn't even happy with the way the paper was written. A few weeks later, he wrote to Schrödinger that the essential thing had been, so to speak, smothered by formalism. Too much mathematics. Not enough plain thought.

But Schrödinger read it, and saw something important. When two particles meet, and then separate, he wrote, they can no longer be described separately, the way they were before. And he gave this connection a name. He said the two had become entangled.

He called entanglement not one, but rather the characteristic trait of quantum mechanics.

Einstein, years later, in a letter to Born, gave it a different name. Spooky action at a distance.

For almost thirty years, it seemed like an argument nobody could ever settle. A question for philosophers. Grey balls, or sealed envelopes? How could you ever tell the difference, if every time you look, you see the same thing?

The answer came from Belfast. From a man named John Bell.

Bell was born in nineteen twenty-eight, into a family without much money. His mother was a dressmaker. His father sold horses. A grammar school was too expensive, so he went to a technical school. And afterwards, he worked for a year as a technician, in the physics department at Queen's University, where the staff lent him books and let him sit in on lectures. His mother, it's said, sewed his university gown from old blackout curtains.

He grew up to work at CERN, in Geneva, designing particle accelerators. But quantum puzzles were his side passion. And in nineteen sixty-four, during a year away in America, he wrote a short paper.

He'd found a way to tell the difference.

If the particles really carry hidden instructions, Bell showed, then when you repeat the experiment many, many times, and compare the results on both sides, the way they match up can only reach a certain limit. No higher. But quantum physics predicted that entangled particles would match up more strongly than that. Past the limit.

So it wasn't philosophy anymore. It was a test. Something you could actually do.

Bell sent his paper to a tiny new journal that lasted only four years. Hardly anyone noticed it. For the first six years, it was cited fewer than a dozen times.

But in nineteen sixty-seven, a young American physicist named John Clauser came across it, drawn by the odd name of the journal. And he decided to try.

People told him he was crazy. That he'd ruin his career. He had almost no money. In nineteen seventy-two, they had their answer. The particles broke Bell's limit. Just as quantum physics said.

Then, in France, near Paris, Alain Aspect made the test even stronger. In nineteen eighty-two, he switched the measuring filters while the particles were already in flight. In a few billionths of a second. Too fast for any message to pass between the two sides, even at the speed of light.

And still, the particles broke the limit.

Every time, the answer was the same. No sealed envelopes. The grey balls are real.

Now, spin is only a name. The electron isn't really a little ball, turning round and round. It's a purely quantum property, with no everyday twin. But it has one very simple feature. When you measure it, you only ever get one of two answers. Up, or down.

And that brings us to Wolfgang Pauli.

Pauli was brilliant, and famous for being sharp with other people's ideas. There was even a legend, told by physicists with great fondness, that laboratory equipment would break whenever he came near. They called it the Pauli effect. It's only a legend. But they loved it.

In nineteen twenty-five, Pauli found a rule. And it's one of the quietest, most important rules in the whole universe. It's called the exclusion principle.

It says that no two electrons in an atom can be in exactly the same state. Each one needs its own place. Its own step on the staircase, and its own spin. Two electrons can share a step, if one spins up and the other spins down. But then that step is full. The next electron has to go higher.

It's a little like a small hotel, where each room holds two guests at most. So as atoms get bigger, and have more electrons, they have to fill the rooms, floor by floor. And that gives every kind of atom its own shape, its own outer floor, its own character.

That's why there is chemistry. Why carbon is not like gold. Why oxygen is not like neon. Why water is wet, and salt is salty. All of it rests on this one quiet rule.

And it's a big part of why you don't fall through the floor.

In nineteen sixty-seven, two physicists, Freeman Dyson and Andrew Lenard, proved something remarkable. Without Pauli's rule, ordinary matter would not be stable. It would collapse into something far, far denser, giving off enormous energy. The rule is what keeps atoms big, and keeps them apart.

Physicists still say that exactly how much of the push you feel from a floor comes from this rule, and how much from ordinary electric forces, is hard to explain simply. But without the exclusion principle, there would be no floors. And no you to stand on them.

In May of nineteen eighty-one, Richard Feynman, the same Feynman who told us to relax and enjoy it, gave a talk about computers.

He'd noticed a problem. Whenever scientists tried to use ordinary computers to calculate what quantum things would do, the work became impossibly large, very quickly. Every extra particle doubled it. And then doubled it again. Even a few dozen particles could be too much for any computer on Earth.

So Feynman suggested something simple, and very bold. If nature is quantum, then build the computer out of quantum things. Let the computer itself be made of parts that obey quantum laws.

And he ended the talk with a line that I think is one of the most cheerful sentences in all of science. Nature isn't classical, dammit, he said, and if you want to make a simulation of nature, you'd better make it quantum mechanical. And by golly, it's a wonderful problem, because it doesn't look so easy. Thank you.

That was the beginning of the quantum computer.

An ordinary computer works with bits. Each bit is a tiny switch, either off or on. Zero or one. A quantum computer works with qubits. Quantum bits. They can be made from all sorts of things. Single atoms. Particles of light. Tiny loops of superconducting metal, like the circuit from the last Nobel Prize.

Now, you'll often hear that a qubit is zero and one at the same time, and so a quantum computer simply tries every answer at once. I'd like to gently set that aside. The physicist Scott Aaronson calls it the most common mistake in explaining quantum computers.

Because if you just set up every possible answer at once, and then look, you get one random answer. Like the electron landing somewhere on the screen. It's no use at all.

The real trick is the one we met at the very beginning, with the double slit. Waves. Adding and cancelling. A good quantum program is arranged so that, for every wrong answer, the waves cancel each other out, like the dark stripes on the screen. And for the right answer, they add up, like the bright stripes. So when you finally look, the right answer is the one most likely to appear.

It's a little like the stripes of light and shadow on that screen. Only now, the bright stripe is the answer to your question.

That's very hard to arrange. And it only works for certain kinds of problems. But for some of them, it could be enormously faster.

In nineteen ninety-four, a mathematician named Peter Shor showed that a quantum computer could break very large numbers into their factors, quickly. That sounds quiet and harmless. But much of the world's online security relies on that being very, very hard. So suddenly, everyone paid attention.
