Now, let's take time dilation a little further, and visit one of the most famous puzzles in physics. It's often called the twin paradox.

A version of it was told by the French physicist Paul Langevin, at a conference in Bologna, in nineteen eleven. It goes something like this.

Imagine two twins. Same age, same birthday. One stays home, on Earth. The other climbs into a very fast spaceship, and travels out to a distant star, at nearly the speed of light, and then turns around and comes home.

For the twin at home, the traveling twin's clocks were running slow the whole time. Their heartbeat, their breathing, their aging. But wait, you might say. From the traveler's point of view, isn't it the Earth that's moving? Shouldn't she see her sibling's clock running slow? Why isn't it the other way around?

That's the paradox. And the answer is that the two twins aren't really in the same situation. The one at home stays in one smooth, steady frame the whole time. The traveler doesn't. She has to speed up, turn around, and slow down again. She feels those changes. Her tea would slide across the table when she turned around. And on the spacetime map, her path is bent, while her sibling's is straight.

And here's the beautiful, surprising part. On the spacetime map, the straight line between two moments is the path with the most time on it. Any bent path, any detour, has less time along it. It's the opposite of what you'd expect from an ordinary map, where the straight road is the shortest. In spacetime, the straight path is the longest-lasting. The wanderer always comes home younger.

This isn't just a story. It happens. We see it every day, high above your head, in the upper air.

When cosmic rays from deep space smash into the top of our atmosphere, they create tiny particles called muons. Muons are like heavy cousins of electrons. And they don't live very long. At rest, a muon lasts only about two millionths of a second, on average, before it falls apart.

That's so short that even moving at nearly the speed of light, most of them should fall apart long before they reach the ground. And yet, lots of them do reach the ground. There are some passing through you right now, gently, harmlessly, as you lie there.

Why? Because they're moving so fast, their clocks run slow, as we see them. Their short lives are stretched out, long enough to reach us.

In nineteen forty-one, two physicists named Bruno Rossi and David Hall measured this, counting muons high in the mountains of Colorado, and lower down, in Denver. They found the fast muons really do live longer than they should.

In the nineteen sixties, two scientists named David Frisch and James Smith carried their instruments to the top of Mount Washington, in New Hampshire, and counted muons there for an hour. And in nineteen seventy-seven, at CERN, in Switzerland, physicists sent muons whizzing around a great storage ring, at almost the speed of light. At rest, those muons would have lived about two millionths of a second. Racing around the ring, they lived about sixty-four millionths of a second. Almost thirty times longer. Exactly as Einstein's equations predicted, to within a fraction of a percent.

Gravity.

Special relativity, the theory from nineteen oh five, is special in a particular sense. It only works for smooth, steady motion. No speeding up, no slowing down, no turning. Galileo's calm ship, on a calm sea.

And now, I'll tell you a small secret about me.

Billionths of a second. In that time, light could barely cross a wide street. But it was there. Time had passed differently for clocks that had simply taken a trip.

Now here's where relativity comes into your everyday life. Maybe you used it today, without knowing.

When you open a map on your phone, and a little blue dot shows you where you are, that dot often comes from GPS. The Global Positioning System. A group of satellites, high above the Earth, about twenty thousand kilometers up. Much higher than my little station. Each one carries atomic clocks, and each one constantly sends out signals saying, here's where I am, and here's exactly what time it is.

Your phone listens to several satellites at once, and from the tiny differences in when their signals arrive, it works out where you are. Light travels about thirty centimeters in a single billionth of a second. So the clocks have to be astonishingly precise. A tiny error in time becomes a big error in distance.

And the satellite clocks are affected by relativity. Twice.

Because the satellites are moving fast, their clocks run slow, by about seven millionths of a second every day. That's special relativity.

But because they're so high up, where the Earth's gravity is weaker, their clocks run fast, by about forty-five millionths of a second every day. That's general relativity.

Put together, the satellite clocks run fast by about thirty-eight millionths of a second, every single day.

That sounds like nothing. But the physicist Neil Ashby, who spent years working on these corrections, wrote that if they were left uncorrected, the errors building up in just one day would cause navigational errors of more than eleven kilometers. After a day, your little blue dot would be in the wrong town.

So the engineers correct for it. Before launch, each satellite clock is set to tick very slightly slow, here on the ground, so that once it's up in orbit, the effects of relativity bring it back to exactly the right rate.

And here's my favorite part. When the first satellite carrying a cesium atomic clock was launched, back in nineteen seventy-seven, not everyone was sure the relativity correction was really needed. It seemed so strange, so abstract. So the engineers built in a switch. The clock could run with the correction, or without it.

They ran it uncorrected, for about three weeks, and measured what happened. The clock drifted, just as Einstein had said it would. Within one percent of the prediction.

Then they switched the correction on. And it has been on ever since.

In the years since, clocks have become so precise that relativity shows up at an almost personal scale.

Einstein's theory made some predictions so subtle that it took most of a century, and some of the most delicate machines ever built, to check them.

One of them is that a spinning mass, like the Earth, doesn't just curve spacetime. It gently drags spacetime around with it as it turns. A little like a spoon turning slowly in a jar of honey, pulling the honey along with it. This is called frame-dragging.

To test it, scientists at Stanford University, with NASA, built a satellite called Gravity Probe B. The idea had first been proposed around nineteen sixty. The satellite was finally launched on the twentieth of April, two thousand and four. It took about forty-seven years from the first idea to the final result.

Inside it were four gyroscopes. Spinning balls, made of quartz, each about the size of a ping-pong ball. And they are, as far as anyone knows, the roundest objects humans have ever made. If one of them were enlarged to the size of the Earth, its highest bump would be only about eight feet tall. Only neutron stars, the collapsed cores of dead stars, are known to be rounder.

The gyroscopes spun, and pointed at a distant guide star, for about a year. And as the satellite circled the Earth, the gyroscopes' directions slowly, very slowly, shifted. Exactly the way Einstein's theory said they should. Both from the Earth's curve in spacetime, and from the tiny drag of the Earth's spin.

The results were announced in May of twenty eleven. Close to the predictions, in both cases.

Near-perfect spheres, spinning in the silence of orbit, quietly feeling the shape of spacetime.

Another prediction was stranger still. If mass curves spacetime, then when very heavy things move violently, they should send ripples through spacetime. Waves, spreading outward at the speed of light. Stretching and squeezing space itself as they pass. Gravitational waves.

Einstein himself doubted they could ever be detected. They'd be far too faint.

But in the United States, scientists built two enormous instruments to try. Together, they're called LIGO. One is in Hanford, in Washington State. The other is in Livingston, in Louisiana. Each has two long arms, set at a right angle, like a giant letter L. Each arm is four kilometers long. Laser light travels up and down the arms, and comes back together, very much like Michelson's experiment on its stone floating in mercury. Only vastly more sensitive.

When a gravitational wave passes, one arm stretches a little and the other squeezes a little, and the light patterns shift. LIGO can sense a change in the length of its arms smaller than one ten-thousandth of the width of a proton. A proton, the tiny particle in the center of an atom.

Einstein's equations reached even further than black holes. They reached the whole universe.
