The year is nineteen fifty. In England, in a philosophy journal called Mind, a paper is published. It's about thirty pages long. And its very first sentence is this.

I propose to consider the question, can machines think?

The man who wrote it was Alan Turing. He was a mathematician. During the war, he'd worked in secret, on breaking codes. And he'd spent years thinking about what a machine could do, in principle, if you gave it the right instructions. But he knew that the question, can machines think, was slippery. What do we even mean by think? He called the original question, too meaningless to deserve discussion.

So he did something clever. He replaced it with a game.

He called it the imitation game. It's played with three people. A man, a woman, and a questioner, sitting in a separate room. The questioner can't see them, or hear their voices. They only pass notes back and forth. And the questioner's job is to work out, just from the written answers, which one is the man and which one is the woman.

Now, Turing asked, what happens if we let a machine take one of those places? If the machine answers the notes, and the questioner can't reliably tell which one is the machine, then perhaps we should stop arguing about whether it thinks.

He even made a prediction. In about fifty years, he wrote, computers would be able to play the game so well that an average questioner would have no more than a seventy percent chance of guessing right, after five minutes of questions. And he thought that by the end of the century, people would talk about machines thinking, without expecting anyone to argue.

In his paper, Turing went through all the objections he could think of, one by one, patiently, like a host answering questions from the audience. And one of them came from a woman who'd lived a hundred years before him. Ada Lovelace.

In the eighteen forties, Lovelace had written about a machine that was never fully built, Charles Babbage's Analytical Engine. And she'd written something very wise about it. The Analytical Engine, she said, has no pretensions to originate anything. It can do whatever we know how to order it to perform.

A machine only does what we tell it. It can't surprise us. It can't create.

Turing took her seriously. But he answered gently, and with a little smile, I think. Machines, he wrote, take me by surprise with great frequency.

And then, near the end of the paper, he had an idea that was really the seed of everything we'll talk about tonight. Instead of trying to write a program that behaves like an adult mind, he asked, why not write one that behaves like a child's? And then let it learn. Give it an education. A child's mind, he imagined, is like a notebook from the stationer's. Rather little mechanism, and lots of blank sheets.

A machine that isn't told everything. A machine that learns.
